Control Theory in FTC Robots

The theory behind what your robot already does. Made for FTC teams who already use PID, a Limelight camera, odometry, and a gyro, but have never seen the ideas underneath. Everything is built on one robot: a DECODE machine named Sparky that shoots artifacts into a goal.

Here is the big secret of this course: you already do control theory every match. When Sparky turns to face the goal and stops, when the flywheel holds a speed so every shot flies the same, when the robot drives to an exact spot on its own, that is control theory at work. You just have not seen its name yet. This course gives you the names and, more important, the reasons. It is 21 short chapters in four parts. The arrows in the map below are the real lesson: each idea is built from the ones that point into it. Each chapter in the list further down is a link, so you can jump straight to any idea.

Who's who in the examples. The stories in this course use one small cast so they read like real team moments. Theo is the programmer, a 13-year-old learning all this (that is you). The driver operates Sparky during the match: feeds and fires artifacts and notices how the robot behaves. Coach Brian is the mentor who asks the good "why does it do that?" questions. And Sparky is the robot.

1. What Is Control?

Control is making a machine reliably do what you want: hit a distance, an angle, or a speed, and hold it there even when the world pushes back.

Prerequisites: none. This is the start.
This chapter: What Is Control?
Unlocks: Chapter 2: Why Control Matters: Robots Are Messy · Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop

Start from things you already control

You already run control systems every day. A control system is anything that makes a machine reach a goal and stay there. Before any names or math, look at three examples. The same simple pattern is hiding inside all three, and that pattern is the whole idea of this chapter.

Example A: a home thermostat. You set the dial to 70 degrees. The furnace turns on when the room is colder than 70 and turns off when the room reaches 70. You picked a goal. A sensor checks the real temperature. The furnace acts to close the gap.

Example B: cruise control in a car. You set the speed to 65 miles per hour. On a flat road the car holds 65. Going up a hill the car slows a little, so cruise control adds gas to bring it back to 65. You picked a goal. A sensor reads the real speed. The engine acts to close the gap.

Example C: Sparky's flywheel. Sparky is our FTC DECODE robot. Its flywheel shooter is a spinning wheel that flings a 5-inch plastic artifact into the goal. Theo, the programmer, sets the flywheel to spin at 3000 RPM (RPM means rotations per minute, a measure of how fast the wheel spins). A motor encoder reads the real wheel speed. The motor acts to close the gap. Every shot flies the same distance only if the wheel holds that speed.

Name the pattern: the three words

All three examples share the same three pieces. These are the words used for the rest of the course, so meet them now with Sparky's flywheel as the example.

  • Setpoint (also called the target): what you want. For the flywheel the setpoint is 3000 RPM. For aiming, the setpoint might be "turn the robot to face 90 degrees."
  • Measurement: what a sensor says is really happening right now. The flywheel's motor encoder might read 2600 RPM.
  • Error: how far off you are. Error is the setpoint minus the measurement. If the target is 3000 and the sensor reads 2600, the error is \(3000 - 2600 = 400\) RPM.

The error is the star of this whole course. When the error is a big positive number, you are far below the target and the machine needs to push hard. When the error is zero, you are exactly on target. The sign matters too: a positive error means "too low, speed up," and a negative error means "too high, ease off."

The core idea: a controller shrinks the error toward zero

Here is the one sentence to remember. A controller is the piece of code that looks at the error and decides how hard to push, so that the error gets smaller and stays near zero. That is the entire job. Everything else in this course is just smarter and smarter ways to do exactly that.

The loop runs over and over, many times per second:

  1. You give the controller a setpoint (the target).
  2. The machine acts (the motor pushes the wheel).
  3. A sensor takes a measurement (the real wheel speed).
  4. The controller compares the measurement to the target to get the error, then adjusts.
Aha: you are not new to control. You have set a thermostat and used cruise control your whole life, and your FTC robot already does this every match. This course just gives the pieces their real names (setpoint, measurement, error) and explains why the tricks work.

What "good control" looks like over time. Watch the real value climb toward the target. When Theo turns the flywheel on, the speed starts at 0, rises fast, and then levels off at the target. A good controller gets there quickly and then holds steady. The picture below shows that shape: the value chasing the flat target line until the gap disappears.

Worked examples

Example 1. Theo sets Sparky's flywheel target to 3000 RPM. The motor encoder reads 2600 RPM. What is the error, and what should the controller do?

Find the error. Error is the setpoint minus the measurement:

\[ \text{error} = 3000 - 2600 = 400 \text{ RPM} \]

The error is positive, which means the wheel is spinning too slowly. So the controller should push the motor harder to speed the wheel up. As the wheel speeds up, the sensor reading climbs toward 3000 and the error shrinks toward zero.

Aha: a positive error always means "below target, push more." A negative error means "above target, ease off." The controller reads the sign and size of the error to decide which way and how hard to act.

Example 2. Sparky needs to aim at the goal. It has no turret, so aiming means rotating the whole robot to face the right way. Theo tells the robot to turn to a heading of 90 degrees. The IMU sensor, which measures heading angle, reads 90 degrees exactly. What is the error, and what should the controller do?

Find the error. Here the target and the measurement are both angles in degrees, not RPM. Error is still setpoint minus measurement:

\[ \text{error} = 90 - 90 = 0 \text{ degrees} \]

The error is zero, so the robot is already pointed exactly where you want it. A good controller does nothing extra here. It just holds the heading. If another robot bumped Sparky to 87 degrees, the error would become \(90 - 87 = 3\) degrees, and the controller would spin the robot back.

Aha: zero error is the goal state. The job is never "keep pushing forever." It is "make the error zero and then hold it there." Notice the units changed from RPM to degrees, but the idea of error did not change at all.

Example 3. Which of these is a control system: (a) a light switch you flip by hand, or (b) Sparky's flywheel holding 3000 RPM by itself? Explain the difference.

Reason it through. A plain light switch is not a control system. It has a goal (light on), but nothing measures the result and nothing corrects it. You flip it and walk away. If the bulb burns out, the switch has no idea and does nothing.

Sparky's flywheel is a control system. It has a goal (3000 RPM), a sensor that measures the real speed, and a controller that acts on the error to hold that speed. When an artifact hits the wheel and slows it down, the controller notices the speed dropped and pushes harder to recover.

Aha: the difference is measuring and correcting. A goal alone is not control. Control means you check the result and adjust. That checking-and-adjusting is exactly the "closed loop" idea you will name in Chapter 3.
Common pitfalls:
  • Error is target minus measurement, in that order. It is setpoint minus what the sensor reads, not the other way around. Getting the order backwards flips the sign, which would tell the motor to push the wrong way.
  • A goal by itself is not control. Setting a target is only step one. Without a sensor measuring the result and a controller correcting the error, it is just a command, not control.
  • Match the units to the quantity. A flywheel target is a speed in RPM. A heading target is an angle in degrees. A drive distance is in inches. Do not mix them up, and remember the error carries the same units as the target.

Practice

P1. Theo sets Sparky's flywheel target to 3200 RPM. The motor encoder reads 3500 RPM. What is the error, and should the controller speed the wheel up or ease off?

Answer

The calculation. Error is target minus measurement: \(3200 - 3500 = -300\) RPM. The error is negative, so the wheel is spinning too fast. The controller should ease off and let the wheel slow toward 3200.

Why it works: the sign of the error tells you the direction to act. A negative error means the measurement is above the target, so you need less push, not more. As the wheel slows, the reading falls toward 3200 and the error moves back toward zero.

Pattern, read the sign first: for any controller, check whether the error is positive (below target, push more) or negative (above target, ease off) before worrying about how much. The size tells you how hard; the sign tells you which way.

P2. Coach Brian points at the classroom thermostat set to 70 degrees and asks Theo to name the setpoint, the measurement, and the error if the room is currently 66 degrees. Then he asks: what is the "machine" that acts?

Answer

The three words. The setpoint (target) is 70 degrees. The measurement is 66 degrees, what the thermometer reads right now. The error is target minus measurement: \(70 - 66 = 4\) degrees. The machine that acts is the furnace. Because the error is positive (the room is too cold), the furnace turns on to warm the room until the error reaches zero.

Why it works: a thermostat is the same three-piece pattern as Sparky's flywheel, just with degrees instead of RPM and a furnace instead of a motor. Naming the pieces in a familiar system makes them easy to spot in the robot.

P3. Theo writes code that sends the flywheel motor a fixed amount of power and never checks the motor encoder. Is that a control system? Why or why not?

AnswerNo. It has a goal (get the wheel spinning) but nothing measures the real speed and nothing corrects it. If the battery sags or an artifact slows the wheel, the code never notices and never adjusts, so the speed drifts and shots miss. It becomes a real control system only once a sensor measures the actual speed and the code acts on the error. That difference, measuring and correcting versus command-and-hope, is the open-loop versus closed-loop idea in Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop.

2. Why Control Matters: Robots Are Messy

A real robot never behaves exactly the same twice. The same command gives a different result every time, and that is the whole reason control exists.

Prerequisites: Chapter 1: What Is Control?
This chapter: Why Control Matters: Robots Are Messy
Unlocks: Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop

Start from a match that went wrong

You already know this feeling. Sparky, your DECODE robot, drives perfectly in practice. Then the real match starts and the same code does something a little different. Before any definitions, watch two moments from one match. The pattern behind both is the whole idea of this chapter.

Moment 1: the same drive command, two different distances. In autonomous, Theo tells Sparky's mecanum drivetrain to run the drive motors at full power for exactly one second, then stop. He wants Sparky to roll 50 inches to the launch spot. At the start of the match, with a fresh battery, Sparky rolls about 60 inches and overshoots. Late in the match, with a tired battery, the exact same command rolls Sparky only about 40 inches and comes up short. Nothing in the code changed. The result did.

Moment 2: the flywheel gets bumped mid-shot. Sparky's flywheel shooter is spinning at a steady 3000 RPM so every artifact flies the same distance. (RPM means revolutions per minute, how fast the wheel is spinning.) Theo feeds a 5-inch artifact into the wheel. For a moment the wheel has to fling that artifact, so it slows down to about 2600 RPM before speeding back up. If the next artifact is fed during that dip, it flies short and misses the goal.

Name the pattern

Both moments share one thing. Something outside the plan changed what the robot actually did. The battery got weaker between the start and the end of the match. An artifact hit the flywheel and slowed it. We have a name for anything like this that pushes the robot away from what you asked for.

Disturbance (plain definition): a disturbance is anything that changes the robot's behavior that you did not command. A sagging battery, extra weight, a bump, friction, an artifact hitting the wheel. It is the difference between what you told the robot to do and what the real world let it do.

Real robots are full of disturbances, and no two are identical, so the same command gives different results. That single fact is why control exists. If robots were perfectly predictable, you could measure the right command once and replay it forever. They are not, so you cannot.

The messiness, part by part

Here are the disturbances Sparky actually meets in a DECODE match. Each one bends the same-command-same-result idea.

  • Battery voltage sags. A battery starts near full and drops as the match drains it. The motors turn the voltage into power, so the same "full power" command gives strong motion early and weak motion late. This is Moment 1 above.
  • Friction changes. Bearings warm up, a wheel picks up dust, a gear meshes a little tighter after a hit. More friction means the same command moves the robot a little less. Friction is never exactly the same twice.
  • The robot's weight shifts. Sparky can hold up to 3 artifacts. Carrying 3 artifacts is heavier than carrying 1, so it accelerates slower and takes longer to stop. The load literally changes between one moment and the next as it collects and shoots artifacts.
  • Floor tiles are slightly uneven. The field is made of foam tiles that never lie perfectly flat. A small ridge at a seam gives the wheels a tiny bump, so a drive that is straight on smooth tile drifts a little over a seam.
  • No two motors are identical. The motors came off a factory line and are close but not equal. Give two of them the same power and one pushes a hair harder, so Sparky curves instead of going perfectly straight.
  • An artifact hitting the flywheel slows it. Every shot briefly steals speed from the wheel, which then has to recover. This is Moment 2 above, and it is the star example of the whole course.

Why "just send the same command" fails

It is tempting to think you can fix all of this with careful measuring. Drive the perfect distance once, write down the exact power and time, and replay it every match. This is called sending a command with no correction, and it is the subject of the next chapter. It fails for one reason: the command that was right a minute ago is wrong now, because a disturbance moved the target under your feet.

Look back at Moment 1. The command "full power for one second" was tuned for a fresh battery and rolled 60 inches. Ten shots later the battery has sagged, and the very same command rolls only 40 inches. There is no single number you can store that is right both times, because the robot is not the same both times. This is the trap:

  • The same power gives more motion on a fresh battery, less on a tired one.
  • The same power moves a light robot (1 artifact) farther than a heavy one (3 artifacts).
  • The same power to two motors curves the robot if one motor is slightly stronger.
  • The same flywheel setting holds 3000 RPM until an artifact hits, then it does not.
Aha: a fixed command assumes a fixed robot. Real robots are not fixed, so a fixed command is guaranteed to be wrong sooner or later. The only thing that stays reliable is checking what actually happened and correcting it.

The fix, previewed

The answer is short, and the rest of the course is how to do it well. Do not trust the command. Measure what the robot actually did, compare it to what you wanted, and correct. Keep doing that many times a second.

Two words worth meeting now, both taught properly soon:

  • The gap between what you wanted and what you measured is the error. Written plainly, error is the target minus the measurement. If you want 3000 RPM and the wheel is at 2600, the error is 400 RPM, and the controller's job is to drive that error back toward zero.
  • Measuring and correcting in a repeating cycle is a closed loop, taught in Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop and Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop. Sending a command with no measurement is an open loop, and it is the one that just failed.

Worked examples

Example 1. In autonomous, Theo runs Sparky's drive at full power for exactly 1.0 second to reach a spot 50 inches away. On a fresh battery Sparky rolls about 60 inches; on a tired battery late in the match the same command rolls about 40 inches. Name the disturbance and explain why one stored command cannot fix both.

The disturbance is battery voltage sag. A fresh battery sits near its full voltage, so the motors turn "full power" into strong motion and Sparky rolls far, about 60 inches, overshooting the 50-inch target. Late in the match the battery has drained, its voltage has dropped, and the very same "full power" is now weaker, so Sparky rolls only about 40 inches and stops short. The figure above shows both landings from the one start line.

Why one command cannot fix both: the command "full power for 1.0 s" is just a fixed instruction. It has no idea how far Sparky actually went. If Theo shortens the time so the fresh-battery run lands on 50 inches, the tired-battery run falls even shorter. If he lengthens it for the tired battery, the fresh-battery run overshoots even more. There is no single time that is right for both, because the robot changed in between.

Aha: the only fix that survives a sagging battery is to measure how far Sparky has actually driven (using two-wheel odometry, in Chapter 5: Sensors) and stop when it has really gone 50 inches, not when a timer runs out.

Example 2. Sparky's flywheel is holding a steady 3000 RPM so every shot flies the same distance. Theo feeds one 5-inch artifact into the wheel. The wheel slows to about 2600 RPM, then climbs back to 3000 over roughly half a second. Sketch what happens to the RPM over time and say why this is a disturbance.

Why it is a disturbance: the wheel was doing exactly what you asked, holding 3000 RPM. Then the artifact arrived and stole some of the wheel's speed to get flung across the field. Nothing in the code asked for a slowdown; the physical world caused it. That is the definition of a disturbance, an uncommanded change in behavior.

Aha: the danger is not the dip itself, it is a shot fired during the dip. A controller that watches the RPM can wait, or push extra power, until the wheel is back at 3000. How fast it recovers is exactly what separates a simple controller from a good one, which is the payoff in Chapter 19: The Flywheel Shooter.
Common pitfalls:
  • A disturbance is not a bug. A sagging battery or an uneven tile is not broken code. It is the normal, expected messiness of a real robot. You do not fix disturbances by writing more perfect commands; you handle them by measuring and correcting.
  • "It worked in practice" is not enough. Practice often runs on a fresh battery on the same patch of floor. The match is different: warmer motors, a tired battery, artifacts on board. A command tuned only in practice will drift in the match.
  • Do not confuse the command with the result. "Full power" is what you asked for. Sixty inches, or forty, is what you got. Control is the habit of never trusting the first without checking the second.

P1. Sparky holds up to 3 artifacts. In autonomous it drives the same "full power for 1 second" command twice: once while carrying 1 artifact, and once while carrying 3 artifacts. Which run travels farther, and what disturbance explains it?

Answer

The answer. Carrying 1 artifact, Sparky is lighter, so the same power moves it farther and it also stops sooner after the power cuts off. Carrying 3 artifacts it is heavier, so it accelerates more slowly under the same power and coasts differently when it stops. The two runs land in different places from the identical command.

Why it works: the disturbance here is a weight shift, the load changing between 1 and 3 artifacts. A heavier robot needs more push to reach the same speed, so a fixed power and a fixed time do not produce a fixed distance. This is the same lesson as the battery example with a different cause: the robot changed, so the result changed.

Pattern, match the fix to the fact that the robot changes: whenever the load, the battery, or the friction can change between two runs, a stored open-loop command cannot stay correct. P3. Theo says: "I will fix the flywheel by finding the exact motor power that gives 3000 RPM, then just always sending that power. No sensor needed." Sparky's flywheel is at 3000 RPM, Theo feeds an artifact, and the wheel dips to 2600 RPM. Explain why his plan fails and what a controller should do instead.

Answer

Why it fails. The stored power was the right amount to hold 3000 RPM only while nothing was disturbing the wheel. The moment the artifact is fed, the wheel drops to 2600 RPM, but Theo's plan keeps sending the same power because it never checks the speed. The wheel will recover slowly on its own, and any artifact fired during the dip flies short. His plan is an open loop: a command with no measurement.

What to do instead. Measure the actual RPM many times a second with the flywheel's motor encoder. Compare it to the 3000 RPM target to get the error (here 3000 minus 2600 is 400 RPM). While the error is large, push more power to close the gap fast; ease off as the wheel nears 3000. That measure-compare-correct cycle is a closed loop, and it brings the wheel back far faster than waiting.

Pattern, never trust a stored command against a disturbance: a fixed power assumes a fixed wheel, but an artifact impact makes the wheel not fixed. Any quantity that can be knocked off target (RPM, distance, heading) needs a sensor plus correction, not a memorized command. This is exactly the open-loop versus closed-loop split in Chapter 3.

3. Open Loop vs Closed Loop

Two ways to make Sparky do something: send power and hope, or measure the result and keep correcting until it is right.

Prerequisites: Chapter 2: Why Control Matters: Robots Are Messy
This chapter: Open Loop vs Closed Loop
Unlocks: Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop

You already choose between these every match

In Chapter 2 you saw the problem: real robots are messy. The battery sags, the tiles are not perfectly flat, and an artifact hitting the flywheel slows it down for a moment. So when you tell Sparky to do something, there are only two ways to go about it. This chapter is the fork in the road for the whole course. Everything after it is one side of this fork.

Watch the same job done both ways before we name anything. The pattern behind both is the whole idea of the chapter.

Way 1: send power and hope. Theo wants Sparky to drive forward 24 inches in autonomous. He writes: run the drive motors at full power for 1 second, then stop. He guessed that 1 second at full power is about 24 inches. He does not check. He just runs it and hopes.

Way 2: measure and correct. Theo wants the same 24 inches. This time he reads Sparky's odometry, the two-wheel tracking system that knows where the robot is on the field. He keeps the motors running and watches the position climb. The instant odometry says he has moved 24 inches, he stops. If Sparky rolls a little extra, he already knows, because odometry told him.

Way 1 is open loop. Way 2 is closed loop. The difference is a single question: does the robot ever check the result?

The core idea: is there a wire coming back?

Open loop means you give a command and never look at what happened. No sensor. No checking. The command goes out to the motor, the motor moves the robot, and the story ends there. The robot has no idea whether it worked. "Run full power for 1 second and hope you went 24 inches." "Spin the flywheel at 60% power and hope it is fast enough."

Closed loop means you measure the result, compare it to your target, and correct. A sensor watches what the robot actually did. You subtract to find how far off you are. That gap has a name we will use all course:

\[ \text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measured value} \]

The controller then acts to make that error smaller, and it repeats, over and over, many times a second. "Use odometry and keep going until you actually reached 24 inches." "Measure the RPM and adjust until it hits 3000." The word "closed" is literal: there is a wire that comes back from the sensor and closes the loop.

Why messy robots need the return arrow

Here is the tie back to Chapter 2. Open loop only works if the world is perfectly predictable, and a robot's world never is. "Full power for 1 second" is 24 inches on a fresh battery on smooth tiles. On a tired battery it is 20 inches. On a scuffed tile it might be 26. Open loop cannot tell the difference, so it drifts: small errors pile up and nobody catches them.

Closed loop fixes itself. Because it measures, it does not care why the number is off. Tired battery, sticky tile, an artifact that just slowed the flywheel: the sensor sees the real result, the error shows up, and the controller pushes until the error is gone. Messiness is exactly the thing closed loop was built to beat.

Aha: open loop trusts a guess. Closed loop trusts a measurement. The robot can only fix a mistake it can see, so the sensor, and the arrow that carries its reading back, is what makes self-correction possible.

Which one does FTC actually use?

Almost always closed loop. This is important, so it is worth saying plainly: open loop is rare in real FTC robots, because the robot never knows if it worked. Any time it matters that Sparky drove the right distance, held the right heading, or spun the flywheel to the right speed, you reach for closed loop. Open loop shows up mostly in this course as the "before" picture, the thing we are improving on. When you use PID, odometry, the gyro, or the Limelight camera, you are already doing closed loop. This chapter just gave it a name. The rest of the course is all about the green "Controller" box: what to put inside it so the correcting is fast and smooth.

Worked examples

Example 1. Autonomous drive to 24 inches. Sparky must drive forward exactly 24 inches off the start line. The battery is a little tired today, so full power for 1 second only moves it about 20 inches, and a scuffed tile can add a couple of inches. Compare open loop and closed loop.

Open loop. Theo commands full power for 1 second and stops. On the tired battery Sparky travels about 20 inches and parks 4 inches short. Nothing measured the distance, so the robot reports success even though it missed. On a different tile it might land at 26 inches and overshoot. The landing spot depends on the day.

Closed loop. Theo reads odometry, which tracks how far Sparky has actually moved across the field in inches. He keeps driving while odometry reads less than 24, and stops the moment it reaches 24. Tired battery? It just drives a little longer to get there. Sticky tile? Same. Odometry measures the real distance, so Sparky lands on 24 inches either way.

Aha: open loop's landing spot is set by the battery and the tile. Closed loop's landing spot is set by the target. That is why autonomous routines use odometry.

Example 2. Flywheel to 3000 RPM. Sparky's shooter must spin its flywheel at 3000 RPM so every shot flies the same distance into the goal. RPM means revolutions per minute, how fast the wheel spins. Feeding a 5-inch artifact into the wheel slows it down for a moment. Compare open loop and closed loop.

Open loop. Theo sets the motor to 60% power and hopes that is 3000 RPM. On a full battery 60% might reach 3000. As the battery drains over the match, the same 60% power spins slower, maybe 2700 RPM, and the shots start falling short. Worse, when an artifact hits the wheel it dips to 2600 for a moment, and open loop does nothing, because it is not measuring speed at all. It just holds 60% power and hopes.

Closed loop. Theo measures the actual RPM with the motor's encoder and compares it to 3000. When an artifact drops the wheel to 2600, the error jumps to 400 RPM, and the controller adds power to bring it back up fast. When the wheel is right on 3000, the error is 0 and it holds steady. The speed recovers after each shot instead of sagging.

Aha: open loop holds a power and hopes it maps to a speed. Closed loop holds a speed by changing the power as needed. When the world pushes back, only the one that measures can push back.
Common pitfalls:
  • Setting a power is not closed loop. "Motor to 60%" is open loop even if 60% happens to give the right speed today. If no sensor is checked, it is open loop, full stop.
  • Closed loop is not "set it and forget it." The correcting happens many times every second, not once. The loop runs the whole time the robot is doing the job.
  • Open loop can still look right, briefly. On a fresh battery and clean tiles, open loop can hit the target by luck. It fails the moment conditions change, which in a real match they always do.
  • The error direction is fixed. Error is always target minus measured value. Keep that order the same everywhere or the controller will push the wrong way. We use this in every chapter from here on.

P1. To aim at the goal, Theo programs Sparky to spin in place at 30% power for half a second, hoping that turns the robot until the shooter points at the goal. (Sparky's shooter is fixed, so aiming means rotating the whole robot.) He uses no sensor. Is this open loop or closed loop, and what will go wrong across a match?

Answer

It is open loop. A command goes out (30% power for half a second) and nothing measures where the robot actually ended up pointing. No sensor, no checking, no correction.

What goes wrong: the robot will not reliably point at the goal. On a fresh battery, 30% for half a second might spin it the right amount. As the battery drains, the same command spins it less, so it stops short of facing the goal. If a wheel catches on a seam in the tiles, it turns even less, and open loop never notices. Because it does not measure the angle, it cannot correct any of this. The aim drifts as the match goes on.

The fix: make it closed loop. Read the Limelight camera's "how far off the goal is" reading, compute error = 0 minus that offset, and keep rotating the robot until the offset is zero, meaning the camera (and the shooter under it) are centered on the goal. Then Sparky lands on the goal no matter the battery or friction. This exact job is Chapter 20.

P2. Sparky's flywheel target is 3000 RPM. Its motor encoder measures 2600 RPM right after an artifact is fed. (a) What is the error? (b) Which way does the controller move the power, up or down? (c) A moment later the encoder reads 3050 RPM. What is the error now, and which way does the power move?

Answer

(a) error = target minus measured = 3000 minus 2600 = +400 RPM. The wheel is 400 RPM too slow.

(b) The error is positive, meaning the wheel is below target, so the controller adds power to speed it back up.

(c) error = 3000 minus 3050 = minus 50 RPM. Now the wheel is a little too fast, so the error is negative and the controller eases the power down to bring it back to 3000.

Notice the same rule handled both cases without any special code: keep error = target minus measured value, and act to shrink it. A positive error means push harder, a negative error means back off. That single rule is the seed of every controller in Part II.

P3. "My autonomous worked perfectly at practice this morning, but at the competition this afternoon Sparky drove past the target every time." The code drives the motors at a set power for a set time, with no odometry. Explain what happened using open loop vs closed loop.

Answer

The code is open loop: a fixed power for a fixed time, with nothing measuring the real distance. Open loop only lands on target when conditions match the conditions it was tuned in.

Why it broke: the routine was tuned in the morning on that morning's battery and floor. By the afternoon something changed. A fresher, fully charged battery is the classic culprit: the same power now spins the wheels faster, so the same time covers more distance and Sparky overshoots. A smoother competition floor would do the same. Open loop cannot see any of this, so it repeats the same command and drives past the target every run.

Pattern, tune-time vs run-time conditions: open loop bakes in the conditions it was set up under, so it fails whenever run-time conditions differ, and at a competition they always differ. Closed loop reads odometry and stops at 24 inches regardless of battery or floor, which is why real autonomous routines are closed loop. The deeper reason batteries and floors matter at all is Chapter 2 (robots are messy); the fix is the feedback loop in Chapter 4.

4. The Feedback Loop

Pick a target, measure where you really are, find the error, correct it, then do it all again. This one loop, run many times a second, is the engine behind every chapter that follows.

Prerequisites: Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop
This chapter: The Feedback Loop
Unlocks: Chapter 5: Sensors: Your FTC Toolkit · Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P)

You already run this loop

Think about pouring a glass of milk up to a line drawn on the side. You do not just tip the carton for a fixed two seconds and walk away. You watch the milk rise. You compare it to the line. If it is low, you keep pouring. As it gets close, you slow down. When it reaches the line, you stop. Your eyes measure, your brain compares, your hand corrects, and you do this over and over until the milk is right where you want it.

That is a feedback loop. In Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop we learned that a closed loop robot measures and corrects instead of sending power and hoping. This chapter opens that loop up and walks through it one box at a time. Every controller in Part II, from the simplest to the smartest, is just a different way of filling in one box of this exact loop.

The one example we will follow: turning Sparky to face the goal

Sparky is our DECODE robot. Its shooter is fixed to the frame, so it cannot swing on its own. To aim, the whole robot has to turn until it points at the goal. Sparky has a mecanum drivetrain, which means it can spin in place without driving anywhere. We want it to face straight at the goal, which for this shot is a heading of 90 degrees. Right now Sparky is turned off to the side. The IMU (also called a gyro, a sensor that measures which way the robot is facing, in degrees) reads that the robot is currently at 75 degrees. So Sparky is aimed 15 degrees short of the goal.

Watch how the robot fixes this. It does not know a magic "spin 15 degrees" command will land perfectly, because the floor, the battery, and friction all vary. Instead it runs the loop.

Walk the loop, one box at a time

Here is the whole loop as a block diagram. Read it left to right, then notice the red arrow at the bottom that carries the measurement back to the start. That back arrow is what makes it a loop instead of a straight line.

Now here is what each box means, defined plainly the first time it appears:

  • Setpoint (target). The value you want. For our turn that is a heading of 90 degrees. The setpoint is your goal, the thing you are steering toward.
  • Measurement. What the sensor actually reads right now. The gyro says the robot is at 75 degrees. This is reality, not what you hoped for.
  • Error. The setpoint minus the measurement. It is how far off you are, and in which direction. For us the error is \(90 - 75 = 15\) degrees. A positive error here means "not turned far enough yet."
  • Controller. The part that looks at the error and decides the correction, meaning how much power to send and which way. For now treat this box as a black box that just says "there is still error, so keep correcting." How it decides is the whole point of the next several chapters.
  • Actuator (motor). The part that does the physical work. Here it is the drivetrain motors, which spin the mecanum wheels so the robot rotates in place.
  • The robot responds. Sparky actually turns a little closer to the goal because the wheels turned.
  • Sensor. The robot's sense organ. The gyro measures the new heading, and that fresh measurement goes back to the compare step to start the loop over.
Aha: the heart of the loop is one subtraction: error = setpoint minus measurement. Everything the controller does is aimed at driving that error to zero. If you can name the target, the measurement, and the error for any robot part, you already understand feedback.

The same loop with our real numbers

Here is the identical loop again, but with the heading numbers filled in so you can see them flow through it.

The key thing to picture: this whole trip happens many times every second. An FTC robot runs this loop very fast, often dozens or hundreds of times per second. So the robot does not leap from 75 to 90 in one giant move. It nudges a little, measures again, nudges a little, measures again. Each pass the error is smaller, so each correction is smaller too, until the robot is sitting on 90 and the error is essentially zero.

Watch the error shrink

Because the loop repeats and each pass shrinks the gap, the error does not vanish in one step. It fades out over many quick passes. If you plot the measured heading over time, you see it climb from 75 up to the 90 degree target and then hold there. The gap to the target, which is the error, gets smaller and smaller.

Aha: feedback does not need to be perfect in one shot. It just needs to shrink the error a bit each pass and repeat fast. Small nudges, checked often, add up to a smooth, accurate landing on the target.

Common pitfalls

Common pitfalls:
  • Getting the error backwards. Error is setpoint minus measurement, target first. If you flip it to measurement minus setpoint, the sign is wrong and the controller pushes the wrong way, driving the robot away from the target instead of toward it. Keep it target minus measurement everywhere.
  • Thinking the loop runs once. The loop is not "turn to 90, done." It runs continuously, many times a second, forever comparing and correcting. That is why a closed loop robot can hold a target even when something bumps it.
  • Confusing the target with the measurement. The setpoint is what you want (90 degrees). The measurement is what the sensor sees (75 degrees). They are different numbers, and the gap between them is the whole reason the controller has a job.
  • Expecting the correction to stay big. As the error shrinks, a good controller eases off. If it kept correcting at full strength right up to the target, the robot would spin past it. That overshoot problem is Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation.

Practice

P1. Sparky's flywheel shooter should spin at a target of 3000 RPM (revolutions per minute) so every artifact flies the same distance. Theo feeds an artifact in and it drags the wheel down, so the motor encoder now measures 2600 RPM. Name the setpoint, the measurement, and the error, and say which way the controller pushes.

Answer

The values. Setpoint = 3000 RPM (what we want). Measurement = 2600 RPM (what the encoder reads right now). Error = setpoint minus measurement = \(3000 - 2600 = 400\) RPM. The error is positive, meaning the wheel is spinning too slow, so the controller pushes to speed the motor up until the error shrinks back toward zero.

Why this is the same loop. The heading turn used a gyro measuring degrees; the flywheel uses a motor encoder measuring RPM. Different sensor, different units, but the boxes are identical: target, measure, error, correct, repeat. Recognizing that any control task is this same loop is the whole point of the chapter.

P2. A teammate writes the error as measurement minus setpoint instead of setpoint minus measurement. For turning Sparky to a heading of 90 degrees while the gyro reads 75 degrees, what error does their formula give, and why is that a problem?

Answer

The calculation. Their flipped formula gives \(75 - 90 = -15\) degrees. The correct formula gives \(90 - 75 = +15\) degrees. The size is the same (15 degrees), but the sign is reversed.

Why it works (and why the sign matters): the controller uses the sign of the error to decide which direction to push. With the correct \(+15\), it spins the robot toward the goal. With the flipped \(-15\), it reads "you have gone too far" and spins the robot the wrong way, so Sparky turns away from the goal and the error grows instead of shrinking. This is one of the most common real bugs in a new control loop.

Pattern, always write error as target minus measurement: keep the setpoint first, everywhere in the code and on paper. A consistent sign convention is what lets the controller trust that "positive error means push forward." Flip it in one place and the loop fights itself.

P3. Sparky's drivetrain is holding a heading of 0 degrees (pointing straight downfield) while it drives. Another robot bumps it and the gyro now reads 8 degrees off to the left. The robot is running its heading loop about 100 times per second. Walk through what the loop does over the next fraction of a second, using the target, measure, error, correct, repeat language.

Answer

The walk-through. Setpoint = 0 degrees. After the bump the sensor measures 8 degrees, so the error = \(0 - 8 = -8\) degrees, which the controller reads as "turned too far left, steer back right." The mecanum wheels nudge the robot right. A hundredth of a second later the gyro measures again, maybe 6 degrees now, so the error is \(0 - 6 = -6\) degrees, a smaller correction. The next pass it is 4, then 2, then back to about 0. Because the loop repeats about 100 times a second, all of this happens in well under a second and looks like the robot instantly shrugging off the bump.

Why it works: this is the payoff of a closed loop that never stops running. The robot did not need to predict the bump. It just kept measuring against its target, and the moment reality drifted from the setpoint, the error became nonzero and the controller corrected it. A disturbance (anything that pushes the robot off target, like a bump or an uneven tile) is handled automatically because the loop is always watching.

Pattern, the loop rejects disturbances for free: you get bump recovery and drift correction without writing any special "if bumped" code. As long as the sensor sees the error, the same target, measure, error, correct, repeat cycle pulls the robot back. This is the single biggest reason FTC robots use closed loop control almost everywhere.

5. Sensors: Your FTC Toolkit

A controller can only fix what it can measure. Sensors are the "measure" box from Chapter 4, and you already own every one of them on Sparky.

Prerequisites: Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop
This chapter: Sensors: Your FTC Toolkit
Unlocks: Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control

Start from the loop you already drew

In Chapter 4 you built the feedback loop: pick a target, measure what is really happening, find the error (target minus measurement), and correct. Then do it again. Almost every box in that loop is code. Only one box is real hardware. The measure box is a physical part bolted to the robot. That part is a sensor.

Here is the one rule that runs through this whole chapter: the controller can only correct what a sensor can measure. If nothing on the robot measures the flywheel's speed, no clever code can hold it at 3000 RPM. It would be guessing, not correcting. So before you control anything, you ask one question: which sensor turns that thing into a number?

Aha: a sensor is the robot's sense organ. It takes something physical (a spin, a turn, a spot on the field, a goal off to one side) and hands the controller a number it can subtract from the target. No sensor, no error. No error, no closed loop.

Good news: you are not buying anything new. A team building a DECODE robot already owns every sensor in this chapter. This is a tour of the toolkit you have. For each tool there is one question that matters: what quantity does it measure? Get that right and the controller half writes itself.

Meet the sensors on Sparky

Here is the team robot, Sparky, from the side, with each sensor called out and the quantity it turns into a number. Trace the loop from Chapter 4 onto the picture: each sensor is a "measure" box, and each one feeds a different controller.

The one rule: match the sensor to the quantity

Look back at the callouts and notice each sensor measures a different quantity: a speed, a heading, a spot on the field, a part's position, an off-center amount. This is the heart of the chapter, and it is a matching game. Name the quantity you want to control, then pick the sensor that measures exactly that:

  • To control a speed, use a speed sensor. Holding the flywheel at 3000 RPM needs a sensor that reports RPM. That is the motor encoder.
  • To control field position, use odometry. Driving to the exact spot on the field where you like to shoot needs a sensor that reports X and Y in inches. That is two-wheel odometry.
  • To control heading, use the IMU. Turning to face a direction, or driving straight, needs a sensor that reports which way the robot faces in degrees. That is the IMU (gyro).
  • To aim at the goal, use the camera. Lining up on the goal needs a sensor that reports how far off-center the goal is. That is the Limelight camera.

If you ever try to control something and the robot just cannot do it, the first question is not "is my code wrong?" It is "am I even measuring the right quantity?" A speed loop fed a heading will never work, no matter how clean the code is.

Here is the whole toolkit in one map. Read each row left to right: the sensor, the quantity it turns into a number, and what Sparky actually does with that number in DECODE.

The sensors, one at a time

Motor encoder: counts rotation, so it gives speed and position

A motor encoder is a sensor built into a motor that counts how much the motor shaft has turned. It clicks off tiny steps called counts (also called "ticks") as the shaft spins. That single skill, counting rotation, is worth two different quantities depending on how you read it:

  • Speed. Counts per second tell you how fast the shaft is spinning right now. That is exactly what the flywheel needs: counts per second becomes RPM (rotations per minute).
  • Position. The running total of counts tells you how far the shaft has turned overall. On an arm or a linear slide, that total becomes the arm's angle or the slide's height. The controller drives the part to a target position and holds it.

Same sensor, two readings: look at how fast the total is climbing and you get speed (for the flywheel); look at the total itself and you get position (for an arm or slide). Speed is "how fast am I turning now," position is "how far have I turned so far."

Aha: the encoder never "knows" RPM directly. It just counts ticks. Your code turns counts-per-second into RPM using how many counts make one turn. This is why a speed loop always sits on top of a motor encoder.

Two-wheel odometry: tracks the robot's spot on the field (X, Y)

Driving to a spot is not the same as spinning a wheel a set number of turns. The tiles are slippery, and Sparky's mecanum wheels can slide sideways. So Sparky does not trust its powered drive motors to know where it is. Instead it uses two-wheel odometry: two small unpowered tracking wheels that roll freely on the floor, plus the IMU. The tracking wheels do not slip the way a pushing wheel does, so they give an honest read of how far the robot really moved.

Put the two tracking wheels together with the IMU's heading and the robot can keep a running tally of its position on the field, written as two numbers: X (how far across) and Y (how far up), both in inches. That (X, Y) pair is exactly the quantity you need to drive to a shooting spot. The FTC library Pedro Pathing uses this position to follow a smooth path across the field (much more on that in Chapter 16).

IMU (gyro): measures heading, which way you are facing

The IMU (short for inertial measurement unit), usually just called the gyro, is a tiny chip on the robot that measures its heading: the direction the robot is facing, in degrees. Think of it as a built-in compass for the match. If Sparky starts facing 0 degrees and you tell it to turn to 90 degrees, the IMU is the sensor that says "you are at 45 now... 80 now... 90, stop."

Notice the quantity: the IMU measures an angle, not a speed and not a position on the field. That is exactly why it is the sensor for a turn and for driving straight. To control a heading you need something that reports heading. The IMU measures the angle directly, so it is the honest answer to "which way am I pointing?" (It also does double duty as part of odometry, which is why odometry is "tracking wheels plus the IMU.")

Limelight camera + AprilTag: measures how far off-center the goal is, and reads the pattern

The camera (a Limelight-style smart camera is common in FTC) is the robot's eyes. On Sparky it is mounted directly under the fixed shooter, pointing the same way the shooter points. That mounting is the whole trick: when the camera is centered on the goal, the shooter is aimed at the goal too. The shooter is fixed and does not swivel on its own, so aiming means rotating the whole robot (mecanum can spin in place) until the camera sees the goal in the middle.

For aiming, the camera does not report a distance in inches or an angle from a compass. It reports one very useful quantity: how far off-center the goal is in the picture. If the goal sits dead center, that number is zero, which is exactly the error you want to reach. If the goal is off to the left, the number is negative; off to the right, positive. That "off-center amount" is a ready-made error for a feedback loop, which makes it perfect for aiming (Chapter 20).

The camera has a second job in DECODE. An AprilTag is a printed black-and-white marker, a bit like a chunky QR code, that the camera can recognize and identify. In DECODE, the obelisk shows the match's random three-piece pattern (the motif), and the robot reads it with AprilTags during the 30-second autonomous period. So the same camera measures a continuous quantity (the off-center amount, for aiming) and reads a discrete label (which pattern, for scoring).

Sensors are a little jumpy (noise)

One honest fact to plant now, because it matters later. Real sensor readings are never perfectly smooth. Even when the flywheel is holding steady, the encoder's speed number might read 2998, then 3005, then 2996, jittering a bit around the true value. This small random wobble in a reading is called noise.

Noise is usually harmless. But one part of a controller reacts to how fast the reading is changing, and noise makes the reading change constantly even when nothing real is happening. That part is the derivative term, and you will meet it in Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD). For now, just file away one sentence: sensors are a little jumpy, and that jumpiness will matter when we get to the D term.

Worked examples

Example 1. Theo wants Sparky's flywheel to hold 3000 RPM so every shot flies the same distance. Which sensor does he need, and why not the IMU?

Match the quantity. The thing being controlled is a speed (RPM). By the one rule, to control a speed you need a speed sensor. The motor encoder on the flywheel counts rotation, and counts per second convert to RPM, so it measures exactly the right quantity.

Why not the IMU? The IMU measures the robot's heading in degrees, an angle for the whole robot. It says nothing about how fast the flywheel is spinning. Feeding a heading into a speed loop gives the controller a number that has nothing to do with its target, so it can never hold 3000 RPM.

Aha: pick the sensor by the quantity you want to control, not by what is lying around. Speed target means speed sensor.

Example 2. During autonomous, Sparky must (a) read the match's random 3-piece obelisk pattern and (b) rotate to face the goal before shooting. Which sensor handles each job?

Both are the camera, doing its two different jobs.

  • (a) Read the pattern: the obelisk shows the pattern using AprilTags. The camera recognizes the AprilTag and reports which pattern it is. That is a discrete label, not a measured amount.
  • (b) Face the goal: the camera reports how far off-center the goal is in its picture. That off-center amount is the error, and the robot rotates its whole body until the number reaches zero. Because the camera is under the shooter, a centered camera means an aimed shooter.

The IMU could hold a heading once you have it, but only the camera can see where the goal actually is and read the pattern. That is why the camera looks out toward the goal.

Aha: one sensor can measure a continuous quantity (off-center amount) for one loop and read a discrete label (the pattern) for a different task. Ask what quantity each job needs.

Example 3. Sparky's flywheel encoder gives 42 counts every full turn of the wheel. Right now it reports 1750 counts in one second. What speed is the wheel spinning, in RPM?

The calculation. First turn counts into turns: \(1750 \div 42 \approx 41.7\) turns each second. Then turns per second into turns per minute: \(41.7 \times 60 \approx 2500\) RPM. So the wheel is spinning about 2500 RPM.

Why it works. The encoder only counts ticks. To get a speed you divide by the ticks in one turn (that converts ticks to turns), then multiply by 60 (that converts per-second to per-minute, which is what RPM means). If the target were 3000 RPM, the controller would now see it is about 500 RPM short and push harder.

Aha: "RPM" is not something the encoder stores. It is counts-per-second, divided by counts-per-turn, times 60. The sensor gives the raw counts; your code makes the RPM.
Common pitfalls:
  • Controlling a quantity you never measure. You cannot hold a speed with only a heading sensor, or drive to a field spot with only a speed reading. Match the sensor to the quantity first, then write the controller.
  • Trusting the powered drive motors for field position. Mecanum wheels slip and slide, so counting their turns drifts. Field position (X, Y) comes from the two unpowered tracking wheels plus the IMU, together called odometry.
  • Thinking a motor encoder measures RPM directly. It counts ticks. Speed comes from counts per second, and you must divide by the counts in one full turn to get real turns.
  • Expecting a perfectly steady reading. Every sensor is a little jumpy. A reading that bounces by a few units around the target is normal noise, not a broken sensor.
  • Confusing the camera's two jobs. The off-center amount (for aiming) is a continuous number that should reach zero. The AprilTag pattern (for scoring) is a label, not something you drive to zero.

Answer

The answer. The sensor is not broken. This small random wobble around the true value is noise, and every real sensor has some. The robot really is holding about 90 degrees.

6. Bang-Bang Control

The simplest closed loop there is. Below the target, full power on. Above the target, power off. It works, it is hard to break, and it never quite sits still.

Prerequisites: Chapter 5: Sensors, Your FTC Toolkit
This chapter: Bang-Bang Control
Unlocks: Chapter 7: Hysteresis, Bang-Bang With a Deadband · Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P) · Chapter 19: Payoff, the Flywheel Shooter

This is the first real controller in the course. Everything before this was setup: what control is, why robots are messy, the difference between open loop and closed loop, the feedback loop, and the sensors that make it all possible. Now you get to meet a real closed-loop controller. It is the simplest one there is, and you have used one your whole life without knowing its name.

Start from two switches you already know

Before any definition, look at two machines that both do the exact same trick. The pattern behind them is the whole idea of this chapter. This is the "you already do this" moment: you have lived with a bang-bang controller in your house forever.

Machine 1: the thermostat in your house. You set it to 70 degrees. The furnace does not have a "half power" or a "gentle warm" setting. It has two states: full-on and off. When the room is below 70, the furnace runs at full power. When the room climbs past 70, the furnace shuts off completely. The room then slowly cools, drops back below 70, and the furnace kicks on again. It cycles on and off all winter. It never holds the room at exactly 70. It just keeps the temperature close, wandering a little above and a little below.

Machine 2: Sparky's flywheel shooter. Your DECODE robot flings 5-inch artifacts into the goal with a spinning wheel called a flywheel. For every shot to fly the same distance, the wheel has to spin at the same speed each time. Say the target is 3000 RPM. RPM means rotations per minute, a measure of how fast the wheel spins. Theo, the team's programmer, writes the simplest thing that could possibly work: if the wheel is spinning slower than 3000, send the motor full power; if it is spinning faster than 3000, cut the power. The wheel spins up, blows past 3000 a little, coasts back down below 3000, gets full power again, and climbs back. Just like the furnace, it cycles. It never locks onto exactly 3000. It ripples around it.

Name the pattern

The furnace and the flywheel run the identical rule. Look only at whether you are below or above the target, and pick one of two outputs:

  • Below the target → full power on.
  • Above the target → power off (or, for some jobs, full reverse).

That is bang-bang control. The name comes from the output slamming between its two limits: bang to full on, bang to off. It is a real closed loop (from Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop) because it measures the real speed with a sensor, compares that to the target, and corrects. It is the simplest closed loop that exists.

The core idea

Every closed-loop controller in this course starts the same way: it computes the error. Error is the target minus the measurement.

\[ \text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measurement} \]

If the wheel target is 3000 RPM and the sensor says it is spinning at 2600 RPM, the error is \(3000 - 2600 = 400\) RPM. A positive error means "you are below the target, speed up." A negative error means "you are above the target, ease off." Every controller you meet from here on tries to drive that error toward zero. What makes each controller different is how it uses the error. Bang-bang uses it in the crudest possible way: it looks only at the sign of the error, plus or minus, and ignores how big it is.

Aha: bang-bang throws away almost everything the error tells it. It does not care whether you are 400 RPM low or 4 RPM low, it just slams to full power either way. That is exactly why it is simple, and also exactly why it can never settle down gently.

The honest catch: it always ripples

Here is the one thing you must remember about bang-bang: it never settles on the target exactly. It always ripples around it. Think about why. The moment the wheel reaches 3000 RPM, the controller cuts power, so the wheel immediately starts slowing down. As soon as it dips below 3000, the controller slams full power back on, so it speeds up and shoots past 3000 again. There is no output that says "hold right here." The only two choices are "speed up hard" and "slow down." So the speed is always either climbing or falling. It draws a small saw-tooth wave: up, over the top, down, under the bottom, up again, forever. This little wobble around the target is the price of the simple rule.

Optional, the rule in one line of code: bang-bang is short enough to write in a breath. In words: power = FULL if (target - measured) > 0 else 0. That single line, run many times a second, is a complete closed-loop controller. No tuning numbers, no gains, nothing to adjust. You will not see another controller this small again.

Why it is worth knowing: the trade-offs

Bang-bang is not just a stepping stone. It is a real tool that real FTC teams really use. Here is the honest scorecard, so you know when to reach for it and when to skip it.

  • Pro, dead simple. One line of code, no math, nothing to compute. If you can read a sensor and flip a motor, you can write it.
  • Pro, no gains to tune. The controllers in later chapters have numbers you must adjust by hand (called gains) until they behave. Bang-bang has zero of those. It works the moment you write it.
  • Pro, robust. When something knocks you far off target, bang-bang instantly gives everything it has. It does not hesitate or get confused. It is very hard to break.
  • Con, it ripples. It can never sit still on the target, so a value you want held steady (like flywheel RPM) always wobbles a little.
  • Con, it is hard on parts. Slamming a motor from full power to off many times a second wears the motor and gears and drains the battery.
  • Con, it is imprecise. That ripple means a mechanism that needs to hold an exact value will always be a bit off, one way then the other.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's flywheel target is 3000 RPM. Right now the encoder reads 2600 RPM. A moment later it reads 3080 RPM. What does a bang-bang controller do in each case, and why does that guarantee a ripple?

Read the sign of the error each time. Error is target minus measurement.

  • At 2600 RPM: error is \(3000 - 2600 = +400\). Positive means below target, so the controller sends full power. The wheel speeds up.
  • At 3080 RPM: error is \(3000 - 3080 = -80\). Negative means above target, so the controller cuts power to off. The wheel coasts down.

Why a ripple is guaranteed. Notice the controller never had a "hold at 3000" option. In the first case it pushes the wheel up, and because it only stops pushing once it is already past 3000, the wheel overshoots. In the second case it cuts power, and because it only turns back on once the wheel is already below 3000, the wheel undershoots. So the speed is always crossing the target on its way up or down, never resting on it. Up, over, down, under, repeat. That back-and-forth is the ripple.

Aha: the ripple is not a bug in Theo's code. It is built into the rule itself. Any controller with only "full on" and "off" must overshoot then undershoot, because it can only react after it has already crossed the line.

Example 2. Mid-match, the flywheel is happily rippling near 3000 RPM. The driver feeds a fresh 5-inch artifact into the shooter. The artifact hits the spinning wheel and drags it down to 2500 RPM in an instant. How does bang-bang react, and is this a good or bad property?

The disturbance and the response. The artifact is a disturbance, an outside event that shoves the robot off target (from Chapter 2: Robots Are Messy). The wheel drops to 2500 RPM, so the error jumps to \(3000 - 2500 = +500\), strongly positive. Bang-bang does the same thing it always does when below target: it slams to full power. Because full power is the most it can ever do, it is already giving the maximum possible push to recover. The wheel climbs back toward 3000 as fast as the motor allows, then returns to its normal ripple.

Good or bad? This is bang-bang at its best. When something knocks you far off target, the crude "just go full power" rule is genuinely a strong recovery. It does not hesitate, it does not compute, it just applies everything it has. This is why bang-bang is called robust: it handles big surprises without any fuss.

Aha: the same bluntness that makes bang-bang ripple near the target makes it recover hard from a big hit. Simple and strong go together here. The weakness and the strength are the same trait.

Example 3. Sparky also has a classifier gate: a small door that opens to let a sorted artifact roll into the ramp and closes to hold the next one back. Theo wants to control it. Is bang-bang a good fit for the gate, and a good fit for the flywheel? Compare.

Ask what the target looks like for each.

  • The classifier gate has really only two useful states: open or closed. There is no "hold the gate 40 percent open and keep it steady" job to do. A control that has just two outputs matches a device that wants just two states. Bang-bang is a great fit. The ripple does not even matter, because "open" and "closed" are the whole point.
  • The flywheel wants to hold a steady value, 3000 RPM, as smoothly as possible, because a rippling speed means shots that fly slightly different distances. Bang-bang can do it, and some FTC teams really do run their flywheel this way. But the ripple is a real cost here: a wheel bouncing between 2950 and 3050 RPM will not shoot as consistently as one locked near 3000.

The rule of thumb. Bang-bang shines for on/off jobs (a gate, an intake, a heater) where the device naturally has two states. It is only "okay" for hold-a-value jobs (speed, angle, distance) where you would rather sit still than wobble.

Aha: match the controller to the job. A two-state controller is perfect for a two-state device and merely acceptable for a hold-still device. That gap is the reason the next chapters exist.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking bang-bang can settle exactly on the target. It cannot, ever. With only "full on" and "off," the value must always be climbing or falling, so it wobbles around the target instead of resting on it. If you need it to sit still, you need a smarter controller.
  • Confusing bang-bang with open loop. Bang-bang is closed loop. It measures the real speed with a sensor and reacts to it. Open loop (Chapter 3) sends a fixed power and never checks. Do not mix them up just because bang-bang looks simple.
  • Ignoring the wear cost. Slamming a motor from full power to off many times a second is hard on the motor, the gears, and the battery. The constant on/off is fine for a slow furnace but can wear parts and waste power on a fast robot mechanism.
  • Assuming "more sensors" or "faster loop" removes the ripple. Reading faster makes the ripple smaller, but never zero. The ripple comes from having only two outputs, not from bad sensing.

7. Hysteresis: Bang-Bang With a Deadband

Plain bang-bang flips on and off the instant you cross the target, so it chatters. The fix is one small idea: leave a gap. Two thresholds instead of one, with space in between.

Prerequisites: Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control
This chapter: Hysteresis (bang-bang with a deadband)
Unlocks: Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P)

In the last chapter you met bang-bang: below the target, full power on; above the target, power off. It is simple and strong. But it has an annoying habit right at the target. This chapter fixes that habit with one small change. It is still bang-bang. It just switches in a smarter place.

The problem: chatter right at the target

Picture Sparky's flywheel again, held near 3000 RPM by plain bang-bang. Remember what happens when the speed is sitting right on the line. The moment it reaches 3000, the controller cuts power, so the wheel starts to drop. The instant it dips below 3000, the controller slams full power back on, so it climbs again. Cross, switch, cross, switch. Because the switch point is the target itself, the controller flips on and off as fast as it can read the sensor, sometimes dozens of times a second, all while barely moving.

That rapid on-off-on-off is called chatter. You have seen your robot do this. A motor buzzing or a relay clicking many times a second, an intake that stutters, a mechanism that hums instead of running clean. Chatter is not just noisy. It is hard on the hardware: slamming a motor from full power to off and back, over and over, wears the gears, stresses the motor, and drains the battery faster than smooth running would.

Aha: chatter comes from putting the switch point exactly on the target. When "turn on" and "turn off" happen at the same spot, the tiniest wiggle across that spot flips the output. There is no rest.

The fix: use two thresholds with a gap

Here is the whole idea of the chapter. Instead of one switch point at the target, use two switch points with space between them:

  • Turn the power ON only when you drop below the LOW threshold.
  • Turn the power OFF only when you rise above the HIGH threshold.

The space between the two thresholds is called the deadband. It is a band where the controller does nothing new. If you are already on and inside the band, you stay on. If you are already off and inside the band, you stay off. Nothing changes until you leave the band out one end or the other.

This tiny change kills the chatter completely. Think about why. To switch off, the wheel has to climb all the way up past the high threshold. Once it is off and coasting down, it cannot switch back on until it falls all the way down past the low threshold. So between any two switches, the speed has to travel across the entire gap. It can no longer flip on a tiny wiggle, because a tiny wiggle does not cross the whole band. The controller switches slowly and cleanly instead of buzzing.

This trick has a name: hysteresis. The word just means "the switch point depends on which direction you are coming from." Coming up, you switch off at the high threshold. Coming down, you switch on at the low threshold. Same wheel, two different switch points, chosen by direction.

A picture of the two thresholds

The clearest way to hold this in your head is to draw the speed as a vertical scale and mark the two switch points on it. Below the low line you turn on. Above the high line you turn off. In between, whatever you were doing, you keep doing.

You already own one: the thermostat

The clearest everyday example is the thermostat in your house, the same machine from the last chapter. A real thermostat does not turn the heat on and off at exactly the number you set. If it did, it would click on and off every few seconds all winter, exactly the chatter problem. Instead it uses a deadband. Say you set it to 70. The heat kicks on when the room drops to 68 and shuts off when the room climbs to 72. Now the furnace runs a good long burst to warm the room from 68 up to 72, then rests a good long while as the room cools from 72 back to 68. It cycles slowly, maybe a few times an hour, instead of buzzing on and off every few seconds.

The honest trade: a wider ripple

Hysteresis is not free. Look again at the first figure. Plain bang-bang chatters, but it stays in a very tight band right at the target. Hysteresis stops the chatter, but the price is a wider ripple band. The value now drifts further from the target before the controller does anything, because it is deliberately ignoring everything inside the gap. On the flywheel, plain bang-bang might hold 2990 to 3010, while a hysteresis version with a 100 RPM gap wanders from about 2950 to 3050. Fewer switches, but a looser hold.

So there is a knob to turn: how big to make the deadband.

  • A wider gap means fewer switches (less chatter, less wear, less noise) but a looser, less precise hold.
  • A narrower gap means a tighter, more precise hold but more frequent switching, creeping back toward chatter.

You pick the deadband size to balance those two. For an intake or a gate, a wide gap is fine, because precision does not matter. For a flywheel that needs steady shots, you want the gap as small as you can make it while still killing the chatter.

Aha: the deadband trades precision for calm. You give up a little accuracy near the target to stop the frantic switching. That is a good trade for on/off jobs and a so-so trade for hold-a-value jobs, which is exactly why the next chapters build something better.

Where this fits on Sparky

Hysteresis is the natural upgrade for any on/off actor on the robot.

  • The intake. Run the intake while Sparky holds fewer than a full load of artifacts, stop when full. A deadband keeps the intake motor from stuttering on and off right at the edge.
  • The classifier gate. The little door that releases a decoded artifact into the ramp. Open and close are its only two states, so a clean deadband switch is perfect and the wider band costs nothing.
  • A simple flywheel. If a team runs the flywheel on bang-bang, adding a deadband stops the motor from buzzing at the target. It shoots a little less consistently than a real velocity controller would, but it runs much calmer than plain bang-bang.

Worked example

Example. Theo sets Sparky's flywheel on hysteresis with a low threshold of 2950 RPM and a high threshold of 3050 RPM (target 3000). The encoder reads this sequence in order: 2900, 2980, 3020, 3060, 3010, 2970, 2940. The motor starts OFF. For each reading, say whether the motor is on or off, and when it switches.

Rule: turn ON only when the reading drops below 2950. Turn OFF only when the reading rises above 3050. Otherwise keep the last state.

  • 2900: below 2950 → switch ON.
  • 2980: inside the gap (2950 to 3050), was on → stay ON.
  • 3020: inside the gap, was on → stay ON.
  • 3060: above 3050 → switch OFF.
  • 3010: inside the gap, was off → stay OFF.
  • 2970: inside the gap, was off → stay OFF.
  • 2940: below 2950 → switch ON.

The motor switched only three times across seven readings. Plain bang-bang, switching at exactly 3000, would have flipped at 3020 (off), 3010 (still above, off), then 2980 and below (on) and more, a switch on almost every crossing. The deadband did its job: far fewer switches. Notice too that the speed was allowed to reach 3060 and 2900, further from 3000 than tight bang-bang would drift. That is the wider ripple you paid for the calm.

Aha: the readings inside the gap changed nothing. That "do nothing in the middle" is the entire mechanism that stops chatter.

Practice

P1. Sparky's intake uses hysteresis on how many artifacts are held: turn the intake ON when the count drops to 0, turn it OFF when the count reaches the full load of 3. Coach Brian asks: could this intake ever chatter on and off rapidly at the switch point? Explain in one or two sentences.

Show answer

But that same gap lets the speed drift further from 3000 (down toward 2950 and up toward 3050) before the controller reacts. Since shot distance depends on wheel speed, a wider speed band means slightly more variation in how far artifacts fly. The calm and the looser hold are two sides of the one deadband: you traded a little precision for a lot less switching.

P3. Coach Brian asks Theo to make the flywheel shots as consistent as possible while still stopping the chatter. Theo can pick the deadband width. Should he make the gap wide or narrow, and what is the limit on how narrow he can go?

Show answer

He should make the gap as narrow as he can, because a narrow gap keeps the speed close to 3000, giving the most consistent shots. The limit is chatter: if he shrinks the gap too far, it gets so close to a single switch point that the wheel can cross the whole (tiny) band on a small wiggle, and the buzzing comes back. So he narrows the deadband until it is just wide enough that normal speed noise does not cross it, and no wider. That is the balance point between precision and calm.

Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking hysteresis settles exactly on the target. It does not. It still ripples, just like plain bang-bang. In fact its ripple band is wider. Hysteresis fixes chatter, not the fundamental wobble. Sitting still on the target is a job for the controllers in the next chapters.
  • Setting both thresholds at the target. If the low and high thresholds are the same number, there is no gap, and you are back to plain bang-bang with all its chatter. The gap has to be real.
  • Making the deadband huge to be safe. A very wide gap kills chatter but lets the value drift far from the target, which can hurt performance a lot. Wider is not automatically better. Use the smallest gap that stops the switching.
  • Getting the direction backward. You turn ON at the LOW threshold (you fell too far, push up) and OFF at the HIGH threshold (you rose too far, ease off). Swapping them would tell the motor to fight the wrong way.

8. Proportional Control (P)

Correct in proportion to how far off you are. Far from the target means push hard. Close means push gently. Right on target means do nothing.

Prerequisites: Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop · Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control
This chapter: Proportional Control (P)
Unlocks: Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation · Chapter 11: Integral Control (PI)

Start from a problem you already have

You already know how to turn Sparky to face a goal. Sparky has a fixed shooter with the camera mounted right under it, so nothing on the robot swivels on its own. Aiming means spinning the whole robot until it points the right way. The mecanum drivetrain can rotate in place, and the IMU tells you the robot's heading in degrees. So "aim" turns into "drive the heading to a target angle." Say the robot is sitting at a heading of 45 degrees and you want it at 90 degrees so it faces the goal.

In Chapter 6 you saw the simplest way to do this: bang-bang control. Bang-bang means full spin power while the heading is below the target, then off (or reverse) once you cross it. It does turn the robot toward 90. But it can only ever do one thing at full strength. So it always spins past 90, comes back, spins past again, and ripples around the target forever. It never sits still. That wobble makes every shot aim a little differently.

Here is the question this chapter answers: how do we make the robot slow its spin as it gets close, so it eases onto a 90 degree heading instead of slamming past it?

The core idea: size the correction to the error

Remember the error from Chapter 4. Error is the target minus the measurement. It is how far off you are right now.

\[ \text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measurement} \]

Bang-bang throws away almost all of this information. It only looks at the sign of the error (am I below or above?) and always pushes at full power. Proportional control uses the whole number. The idea is one sentence:

The whole idea: make the correction proportional to the error. Big error, big correction. Small error, small correction. Zero error, zero correction. "Proportional" just means "sized in step with," so as the error shrinks, the push shrinks with it.

Watch it happen as the robot turns. Target heading is 90 degrees.

  • The robot starts at a heading of 45 degrees. The error is \(90 - 45 = 45\) degrees. That is a big error, so P applies a big correction and the robot spins fast.
  • As it turns, the error keeps shrinking, so the push keeps easing off. The robot naturally slows its spin as it approaches.
  • Near the end the heading is 85 degrees. The error is only \(90 - 85 = 5\) degrees. That is a small error, so P applies a small correction and the robot just creeps the last bit.
  • At a heading of 90 degrees the error is 0, so the correction is 0. The wheels ease to a stop right where you wanted them.

This is the exact opposite of bang-bang. Bang-bang pushes just as hard at 5 degrees away as at 45 degrees away, which is why it overruns. Proportional control lets off the gas on its own as it arrives.

Picture the correction shrinking with the error

The clearest way to feel proportional control is to draw the push as an arrow. The arrow points toward the target heading, and its length is the size of the correction. Far from 90 the arrow is long. Close to 90 it is short. At 90 there is no arrow at all.

The gain: how strongly it reacts (optional math aside)

There is one number that decides how aggressive proportional control is. It is called the gain, written \(K_p\). The gain sets how much correction you get for each degree of error. A big gain means a hard reaction to even a small error. A small gain means a soft, lazy reaction.

Optional formula (you can skip this and lose nothing): \[ \text{correction} = K_p \times \text{error} \] That is the entire rule for a proportional controller. Multiply how far off you are by the gain, and that is how hard you push. Everything in this chapter is just this one line in words.

You do not compute this by hand during a match. The robot does the multiply every loop, dozens of times a second (that loop is the feedback loop from Chapter 4). Your job as the programmer is to pick a good \(K_p\). That job has a name, tuning, and it gets its own chapter (Chapter 14). For now, just hold the picture: \(K_p\) is the knob for "how strongly does Sparky react."

Two honest facts about P (do not skip these)

Proportional control is smoother than bang-bang. But it is not magic, and it is not perfect. There are two things it does that you need to know, because they are the reason the next chapters exist.

Honest fact 1: too much gain brings back the wobble

It is tempting to crank \(K_p\) way up so the robot snaps to a 90 degree heading as fast as possible. But if the gain is too high, the correction stays large even when you are close, so the robot carries too much spin speed and blows past 90. Then it corrects hard the other way and blows past again. You are back to overshooting and oscillating, the same wobble bang-bang had.

So more gain is not always better. There is a sweet spot. This whole trade-off is the topic of the next chapter, Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation.

Honest fact 2: P alone often leaves a small leftover gap

This one surprises people. Leave the spinning robot for a moment and think about Sparky's arm instead. The arm swings up on a motor, and a motor encoder reads its angle. The arm has to hold up against gravity, which is always pulling it down. Say you command the arm to 90 degrees with proportional control.

As the arm rises, the error shrinks, so the correction shrinks too. But gravity does not shrink. It keeps pulling with the same force the whole time. At some point the arm reaches an angle, say 85 degrees, where the small proportional push exactly balances gravity. There the arm stops rising. The error is still 5 degrees, but that leftover error only produces a tiny push, and that tiny push is all it takes to match gravity. So the arm just sits there, 5 degrees short, forever.

That leftover gap has a name: steady-state error. It is the small, constant miss that proportional control settles into when something (gravity, friction, a constant drag) keeps pulling. P shrinks the error but cannot quite close it, because a zero error would mean zero push, and zero push cannot hold against a constant load.

Notice: the spinning robot in the first figure had almost no leftover gap, but the arm here does. The difference is the constant pull. A robot rotating on the floor has little steady force fighting it, so P gets very close to the target heading. An arm fighting gravity always has one, so P leaves a gap. The lesson: P leaves a steady-state error whenever a constant load pushes back. The fix is the integral term, coming in Chapter 11: Integral Control (PI).

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky is turning to a target heading of 90 degrees. At one moment the IMU reads 50 degrees. A moment later it reads 80 degrees. Using proportional control, is the drivetrain spinning harder at 50 degrees or at 80 degrees, and why?

Find the error at each moment. Error is target minus measurement.

  • At a heading of 50 degrees: error \(= 90 - 50 = 40\) degrees.
  • At a heading of 80 degrees: error \(= 90 - 80 = 10\) degrees.

Compare. Proportional control makes the push proportional to the error. The error at 50 degrees (40) is four times the error at 80 degrees (10). So the correction at 50 degrees is four times as strong. The robot spins harder at 50 degrees and much more gently at 80 degrees.

Aha: the controller does not need a separate rule for "slow down near the end." Slowing down falls out automatically, because a smaller error simply produces a smaller push.

Example 2. Coach Brian says, "Turning to face the goal with bang-bang keeps wobbling on the heading, so shots miss. We switched to proportional control and it stopped wobbling. But now, on matches where the battery cable snags and tugs the robot one way, it stops about 3 degrees short of the target heading every time." What are the two behaviors here, and what causes the 3-degree miss?

Behavior 1, the wobble went away. Bang-bang always pushed at full power, so it overshot and rippled around the target heading. Proportional control eases off as the robot nears the heading, so it stops wobbling and settles. That is the win.

Behavior 2, the 3-degree miss. The snagging cable is a constant tug, like gravity on the arm. As the robot nears the heading the proportional push shrinks. It shrinks until it is just barely strong enough to fight the cable tug, and there the robot stops. The leftover 3-degree error still exists, but it only makes a tiny push, and that tiny push is exactly canceled by the tug. So the robot sits 3 degrees short.

Aha: that 3-degree miss is steady-state error. It is not a bug in your code. It is what P does whenever a constant force fights back. The cure is not more \(K_p\) (that just brings back wobble); it is adding an integral term, in Chapter 11.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking "bigger gain is always better." Turning \(K_p\) up makes the robot snap faster, but past a point it overshoots and oscillates, right back to the bang-bang wobble. There is a sweet spot, not "more is better." (See Chapter 9.)
  • Expecting P alone to land perfectly. When a constant load pulls (gravity, friction, a snagging cable), P settles a little short and stays there. That leftover is steady-state error, and it is normal. Do not try to fix it by cranking the gain.
  • Forgetting the sign of the error. If the robot overshoots to a heading of 95 degrees, the error is \(90 - 95 = -5\) degrees, a negative number. The correction becomes negative too, so P automatically spins the robot back the other way. The same one rule handles both directions.

Answer

The calculation. Error is target minus measurement. At a heading of 60 degrees the error is \(90 - 60 = 30\) degrees. At 84 degrees the error is \(90 - 84 = 6\) degrees. The first error is five times the second.

Why it works: proportional control sizes the push in step with the error. Five times the error means five times the push. So the robot is spinning about five times harder at a heading of 60 degrees than at 84 degrees, which is exactly why it turns quickly at first and only creeps at the end.

P3. Sparky's arm is commanded to 90 degrees using proportional control only. Gravity pulls the arm down the whole time. 9. Overshoot and Oscillation

Push the correction too hard and the robot swings past the target, then wobbles back and forth around it before it settles.

Prerequisites: Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P)
This chapter: Overshoot and Oscillation
Unlocks: Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD): The Anticipating Brake

You have seen your robot do this

Theo is tuning how Sparky aims. Remember that Sparky's shooter is fixed. It is bolted straight to the frame, and the camera sits right under it facing the same way. So to point the shooter at the goal, Sparky spins its whole body in place until it faces the right direction. Aiming is just turning the robot to a heading. The IMU (the gyro that reports which way the robot faces) reads that heading in degrees.

Theo tells Sparky to rotate from heading 0 degrees to heading 90 degrees to face the goal. In Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P) he learned the rule: push harder when you are far from the target, and ease off as you get close. So he turns the strength of that push way up to make the robot snap to the heading fast.

It does not snap. The robot spins all the way to 100 degrees, past the 90 he asked for. Then it jerks back to 82. Then to 96. Then to 86. The robot wobbles back and forth for a full second before it finally holds at 90. Every artifact Theo fires during that wobble misses, because the whole robot is still swinging when the artifact leaves the shooter.

Coach Brian walks over. "Why does it wobble?" the driver asks. That question is this whole chapter.

The core idea: two things just happened

Two different problems showed up in that one test. They have names, and you will use both names for the rest of this course.

  • Overshoot is going past the target before coming back. The robot was told to reach heading 90 degrees, but it flew to 100 first. That extra 10 degrees past the target is the overshoot.
  • Oscillation is wobbling back and forth around the target. After the robot passed 90, it swung back below it, then above it, then below, like a swing losing height. Each swing was smaller than the last until it finally stopped on 90.

Here is the key picture for the whole chapter. It shows the same move, "spin to heading 90 degrees," done three times with three different push strengths. In Chapter 8 you learned the push strength has a name: the proportional gain (often written \(k_P\)), the number that decides how hard the controller pushes for a given error. Watch how the heading changes over time in each case.

Why high gain causes it

Think about what the controller is doing at the exact moment the robot reaches heading 89 degrees, almost at its 90 degree target. The error, which is the target minus the current heading, is only 1 degree. A gentle controller would give a gentle nudge here, because the error is tiny.

But a high gain controller multiplies even a small error by a big number. So at 89 degrees it is still shoving the robot hard. The robot is already spinning fast toward 90. It has momentum, which is the tendency of a moving thing to keep moving, and a whole robot is heavy, so it carries a lot of it. The hard shove plus that momentum carry it straight past 90 to 100. That is the overshoot.

Now the robot faces heading 100, which is 10 degrees past the target. The error flips sign: the target is now behind it. The high gain controller sees that big error and shoves hard in the other direction. The robot spins back, and its momentum carries it past 90 again, this time down to 82. Then it shoves back the first way. Each swing is a little smaller, because friction bleeds off some energy every time, so the wobble shrinks and finally dies out on 90. That back and forth is the oscillation.

Aha: overshoot and oscillation are not a bug in your code. They are what happens when the controller keeps pushing hard while the robot is already almost there. The correction is too strong for how close you are.

The everyday version: yanking the wheel

You do not need a robot to feel this. Imagine steering a car down the middle of a lane. The middle of the lane is your target, like the 90 degree heading. If you nudge the wheel gently, you glide back to the center. But if you yank the wheel hard every time you drift a little, you shoot across the center to the other side of the lane. Then you yank back and shoot across again. You weave back and forth. That weaving is oscillation, and yanking the wheel is high gain.

Filling a glass of water is the same story with overshoot alone. If you turn the tap on full blast to fill it fast, the water rushes past the line you wanted and spills over the top before you can shut it off. Turn the tap gently and you stop right at the line. Fast and hard means overshoot. Gentle means you land on target.

New words you will use from here on

When engineers look at a response curve like the one above, they measure four things. Learn these names now, because every later chapter uses them to say whether a controller is good.

  • Rise time: how fast the robot first reaches the target. A short rise time means it gets there quickly. Low gain has a long, lazy rise time.
  • Overshoot: how far past the target it goes on the first swing, measured from the target. The robot's overshoot was 10 degrees (it hit heading 100 aiming for 90).
  • Settling time: how long it takes for the wobble to die down and stay near the target. High gain has a long settling time because it oscillates for a while.
  • Steady-state error: the leftover gap between where it finally rests and the target it was given. If the robot settles at heading 89 when you asked for 90, the steady-state error is 1 degree.

These four fight each other. Turn the gain up and the rise time gets shorter (good), but the overshoot and settling time get worse (bad). Turn it down and the wobble goes away (good), but now it is slow and may never fully arrive (bad). This picture shows all four on one heading run with the gain set too high.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky is told to spin from heading 0 degrees to heading 90 degrees to face the goal. With the gain set high, it swings to 100 degrees, back to 82, up to 96, down to 86, and finally holds at 90. Name what is happening and explain why.

Read the numbers. The target is 90 degrees. The robot's first swing reaches 100, which is 10 degrees past the target. That 10 degrees is the overshoot. After that it goes 82, 96, 86, then 90. Those numbers sit above the target, then below, then above, then below, in shrinking swings. That back and forth is the oscillation.

Why it happens. With a high gain, the controller multiplies even a tiny error by a big number, so it is still pushing hard when the robot is almost at 90. The whole robot is also already spinning, so its momentum plus the hard push carry it past the target. Once it is past, the error flips direction and the controller shoves hard the other way, which sends it past again. Friction shrinks each swing, so the wobble eventually dies out on 90.

Pattern, high gain trades speed for wobble: a strong push gets you to the target fast (short rise time) but keeps pushing when you should be easing off, which causes overshoot and a long settling time. Whenever you see a value swing past its target and then bounce around it, suspect the gain is too high. The fix is not just "lower the gain," which makes it slow. The real fix is a part that eases off as you approach, which is the derivative term in Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD).

Example 2. Sparky's flywheel shooter must hold 3000 RPM (revolutions per minute) so every shot flies the same distance. Theo starts it with a high gain controller. The wheel races to 3300 RPM, drops to 2750, climbs to 3150, dips to 2900, then holds near 3000. What went wrong, and why do shots taken in the first second miss?

Read the numbers. The target is 3000 RPM. The wheel first hits 3300, which is 300 RPM past the target. That is overshoot, in RPM this time instead of degrees. Then it bounces 2750, 3150, 2900 around 3000 in shrinking swings, which is oscillation. It settles near 3000 only after several swings, so the settling time is long.

Why it happens. This is the same story as the heading, just with speed instead of angle. A spinning wheel has a lot of momentum. When the controller pours full power to reach 3000 fast, the wheel's momentum carries the speed past 3000 to 3300. The controller then cuts power hard, the speed drops below 3000, and the cycle repeats. An artifact shot at 3300 RPM flies too far. An artifact shot at 2750 RPM falls short. So any shot taken while the wheel is still bouncing lands in the wrong place. Only after the RPM settles are the shots repeatable.

Pattern, overshoot is measured in the unit of the thing you control: for the heading it was degrees, for the flywheel it is RPM, for a drivetrain it would be inches. The story is always the same. A strong correction plus the machine's momentum carries the value past the target, then it wobbles back. This is why the flywheel is the star example of this course. You will see the full fix for it in Chapter 19: Payoff: The Flywheel Shooter.

Example 3. Coach Brian shows Theo two response curves for the same "spin to heading 90 degrees" command. Curve A reaches 90 in half a second with no overshoot but then creeps and settles at 87. Curve B reaches 90 in a tenth of a second but overshoots to 105 and wobbles for a full second. Using the four words (rise time, overshoot, settling time, steady-state error), describe each and say which tuning you would ship for a match.

Describe curve A. Longer rise time (half a second). No overshoot. Short settling time, because there is no wobble to settle. But a steady-state error of 3 degrees, because it rests at heading 87 instead of 90.

Describe curve B. Very short rise time (a tenth of a second). Big overshoot (15 degrees, since it hit 105). Long settling time (a full second of wobble). Its steady-state error is small once it finally settles.

Which to ship. In a match the robot must be pointing at the goal and holding still at the moment you shoot. Curve B is fast but is still wobbling when you want to fire, so shots taken early will miss. Curve A is steady but points 3 degrees off, which could also miss a far goal. Neither is good enough alone. The honest answer is that plain P forces this trade, and you should not pick one bad option. You want fast rise and no wobble and no leftover gap, which needs the extra terms coming in the next chapters.

Aha: "just turn the gain up" is a trap. More gain buys speed but sells it back as overshoot and wobble. The rest of this course is about getting the speed without paying the wobble.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking more gain is always better. A bigger push reaches the target faster, but past a point it only adds overshoot and wobble. Fast is not the same as good.
  • Confusing overshoot with oscillation. Overshoot is going past the target once. Oscillation is wobbling around it many times. A response can overshoot a little and then settle without really oscillating, or it can oscillate for a long time. They often show up together, but they are different measurements.
  • Blaming your code. Wobble usually is not a logic bug. It is a tuning problem. The controller is doing exactly what you told it, just too hard.
  • Firing before it settles. In a match, wait for the robot or flywheel to stop wobbling before you shoot. A value that is still swinging is not at its target yet, even if it passes through the target on the way.

10. Derivative Control (PD): The Anticipating Brake

Watch how fast the error is shrinking and ease off early, so you slow down before the target instead of sailing past it.

Prerequisites: Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation
This chapter: Derivative Control (PD): The Anticipating Brake
Unlocks: Chapter 12: PID Together

Where we left Theo

At the end of Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation, Theo was stuck. He told Sparky to spin from a heading of 0 degrees to a heading of 90 degrees so it would face the goal. The shooter is bolted straight onto the frame and cannot swivel by itself. To aim, the whole robot rotates in place on its mecanum wheels until it points the right way, and the IMU reports the heading in degrees. With a strong proportional push the robot swung to 90 fast, but it flew past to 100, then wobbled back and forth for a whole second before it held still. Every shot the driver fired during that wobble missed.

Theo tried the other direction and turned the push down. Now the robot did not wobble. But it crawled to 90 so slowly that the match was half over before it got there. Fast and wobbly, or steady and slow. Theo could not get both.

Coach Brian gave him a hint that sounds like it has nothing to do with robots. "How do you stop your bike at a stop sign?" Coach Brian asked. "Do you pedal full speed right up to the line and then slam the brakes?"

You already do this: the stop sign

Think about walking fast toward a door, or riding a bike up to a stop sign. You do not run full speed right up to the line and then jam to a halt. You would fly past it. Instead you start slowing down early, while you are still a few feet away. You ease off so that you arrive already slow, and you stop right on the line.

How do you know when to start slowing? You do not just look at how far away the line is. You also feel how fast you are closing in on it. If the line is far but you are barely moving, you keep going. If the line is close and you are rushing at it fast, you brake hard, right now. You are reacting to your speed toward the target, not only to your distance from it.

The core idea: react to how fast the error is changing

Here is the piece that fixes Theo's turn. Back in Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P), the proportional term looked at the error, which is the target heading minus the heading the robot is pointing right now. It pushed harder when the error was big. That is like braking based only on how far the stop sign is. It never thinks about your speed.

The derivative term adds the missing piece. It does not look at how big the error is. It looks at how fast the error is changing. In plain words: is the gap between the robot's heading and the target closing quickly, slowly, or getting bigger?

  • If the robot is spinning fast toward 90 degrees, the error is shrinking fast. The derivative term sees that and pushes back, like a brake, so the robot slows down before it arrives.
  • If the robot is barely turning, the error is shrinking slowly. The derivative term does almost nothing and lets the proportional push do its job.
  • If the robot is drifting the wrong way and the error is growing, the derivative term helps push toward the target instead.

That is why the chapter calls it the anticipating brake. It anticipates, which means it acts on what is about to happen. It sees that you are closing in fast and eases off before you get there. When you add this derivative brake to the proportional push, the controller is called PD: proportional plus derivative.

Optional one-line math (you can skip this and lose nothing): \[ \text{output} = k_P\,e + k_D\,\frac{\Delta e}{\Delta t} \] Here \(e\) is the current error and \(\frac{\Delta e}{\Delta t}\) is how fast that error is changing. The first part, \(k_P\,e\), is the proportional push from Chapter 8. The new part, the \(k_D\) term, goes negative when the error is shrinking fast, which is the brake. That is the whole idea, and the intuition above is what matters.

What it looks like on Sparky

Theo runs the same test again: rotate the whole robot from a heading of 0 degrees to 90 degrees. First he runs it with proportional push alone, turned up high, the wobbly setup from Chapter 9. Then he adds a derivative term and runs the exact same command. Here are both headings over time on one graph.

Look at what changed. The green PD curve climbs nearly as fast as the red one at the start, when the robot is far from its target heading and spinning quickly toward it. But near 90 the two curves split. The red one keeps shoving and blows past. The green one feels itself rushing at the target and brakes, so it eases in and stops on 90. Theo did not have to slow the whole turn down. He only slowed the ending.

Aha: proportional asks "how far am I from the target?" Derivative asks "how fast am I closing in?" Together they let you charge toward the target and still stop cleanly on it, exactly like a good driver braking before a stop sign.

The honest cost: derivative amplifies noise

The derivative term is not free. It reacts to how fast the measurement is changing. That is great when the change is real, like the robot truly spinning toward 90. But real sensors are not perfectly smooth. An IMU heading reading jumps around a little from one instant to the next, even when the robot is sitting still. Those tiny, fast jumps are called noise.

To the derivative term, a tiny fast jump in the reading looks exactly like a fast change. So it reacts hard to it. A heading reading that flickers up 2 degrees and down 2 degrees very quickly makes the derivative term slam the output up and down, even though the robot never really moved. The result is a jittery, buzzing output. Here is the problem drawn out.

This is why you cannot just crank the derivative gain up to get a perfect brake. A little derivative smooths the landing. Too much turns sensor noise into a violent, twitchy output. In Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller you will see the rule of thumb: raise the derivative gain until the wobble is gone, then back off the moment the robot starts to buzz or twitch. Teams also clean up the sensor reading first, so the derivative has smoother data to work with.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky is told to rotate from a heading of 0 degrees to 90 degrees. With a strong proportional push alone it flies to 100 degrees, then wobbles: 82, 96, 88, and finally holds at 90. Theo adds a derivative term and runs the same command. Now it rises to 90, slows down as it nears 90, barely tips to 91, and holds. Explain, in terms of the error and how fast it is changing, why the second run does not overshoot.

Read the runs. First run: proportional alone. It overshoots to 100 (10 degrees past the 90 target) and then oscillates around 90 before settling. Second run: PD. It rises about as fast but eases into 90 with almost no overshoot (just 1 degree, to 91).

Why the derivative fixes it. In the first run the controller only looks at the error, which is the target minus the current heading. At 89 degrees the error is only 1 degree, but a strong proportional push still shoves hard, and the robot's spinning momentum carries it past 90 to 100. In the second run the derivative term also watches how fast the error is shrinking. As the robot races up toward 90, the error is shrinking very fast, so the derivative term pushes back like a brake and cuts the output before the robot arrives. The robot slows into 90 instead of blowing through it, so there is no overshoot to wobble back from.

Pattern, derivative brakes on the approach speed: whenever a value rushes at its target and overshoots under P alone, adding a derivative term slows the ending without slowing the whole move. Reach for D whenever the fix you want is "get there fast but stop cleanly." It anticipates the arrival and eases off early, the same move you make braking for a stop sign.

Example 2. The driver complains that Sparky buzzes and jerks slightly even when it is just holding still on the goal, aimed and waiting to shoot. Theo had turned the derivative gain up very high to kill the overshoot from the last match. What is happening, and what should he do?

What is happening. The robot is holding still, so the real error is basically zero and should stay that way. But the IMU heading reading is not perfectly smooth. It jitters a few tenths of a degree up and down very quickly, which is normal sensor noise. The derivative term reacts to how fast the reading changes, and those tiny jitters change fast, so the derivative term reads them as fast motion and slams the motor output up and down. That is the buzzing and twitching the driver hears. Nothing is actually turning the robot; the controller is chasing noise.

What to do. Turn the derivative gain back down. It was set too high. Lower it until the buzzing stops but there is still enough braking to prevent overshoot on a real move. Teams also smooth the sensor reading before feeding it to the derivative term, so small jitters do not turn into big spikes.

Pattern, twitchy output means too much derivative: a robot that buzzes or jerks while holding still is almost always a derivative-gain-too-high symptom, because D is amplifying sensor noise. This is the boundary of the derivative term: a little smooths the landing, too much turns noise into jitter. Contrast it with too much proportional, which causes a slow back-and-forth wobble, not a fast buzz.

Example 3. Coach Brian draws two response curves for the same "rotate the robot to a heading of 90 degrees" command. Curve A rises fast, overshoots to 103, and wobbles four times before settling on 90. Curve B rises almost as fast, eases in near the top, and settles on 90 with no visible overshoot and no wobble. Which curve has a derivative term added, and name the one part of the move where the two curves behave differently?

Which is which. Curve B has the derivative term. Overshoot and repeated wobble are the signature of proportional push alone (Chapter 9). Easing cleanly into the target with no overshoot is the signature of adding a derivative brake.

Where they differ. At the very start, when the robot is far from 90 and spinning fast, the two curves look almost the same, because the error is large and the proportional push dominates in both. They only split near the top, on the approach to 90. There the error is shrinking fast, so Curve B's derivative term brakes and eases it in, while Curve A keeps pushing and blows past. The derivative term does its main work on the approach, not at the start.

Aha: the derivative term barely changes the fast early part of a move. It earns its keep at the end, on the approach, where it turns a crash-past into a gentle landing.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking derivative reacts to the size of the error. It does not. That is the proportional term's job. Derivative reacts only to how fast the error is changing. A big error that is not moving gets no reaction from D.
  • Cranking the derivative gain up to "brake harder." Past a point, more derivative does not help. It just amplifies sensor noise and makes the output buzz and twitch. More is not better here either.
  • Blaming the motor for a buzz. A twitchy, buzzing output while the robot holds still is usually too much derivative gain reacting to noisy sensor readings, not a broken motor.
  • Expecting D to remove the leftover gap. The derivative term cuts overshoot and wobble. It does not erase the small steady-state error that P alone leaves. That leftover gap needs the integral term in Chapter 11: Integral Control (PI).

★ Landmarks it points toward

  • Damping: the general name for anything that shrinks a wobble. The derivative term adds damping on purpose, on top of the friction the robot already has.
  • Derivative kick: a sudden jump in the output that happens when you change the target suddenly, because the error changes instantly. Real controllers dodge it by taking the derivative of the measurement instead of the error.
  • Low-pass filter: the standard tool teams use to smooth a noisy sensor reading before the derivative term sees it, so noise does not become jitter.
  • Derivative on measurement: the common trick of computing the rate of change from the sensor reading rather than the error, which avoids the derivative kick above.

11. Integral Control (PI)

Add up the leftover error over time and keep pushing until the gap is truly gone.

Prerequisites: Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P)
This chapter: Integral Control (PI)
Unlocks: Chapter 12: PID Together

Start from the gap P leaves behind

In Chapter 8 you met one honest problem with proportional control. When something keeps pulling on the robot, like gravity or friction, P settles a little short of the target and just sits there. That leftover gap has a name: steady-state error. It is the small, constant miss P cannot close.

Here is why P gets stuck. Remember the P rule: the push is sized to the error. Big error means big push. Small error means small push. Now picture Sparky's arm commanded to 90 degrees. The arm is the part that lifts and holds. A motor encoder counts the motor's turns, so the code always knows the arm's angle. Gravity pulls the arm down the whole time. As the arm rises, the error shrinks, so the push shrinks with it. The arm keeps rising until the push has shrunk to exactly match gravity, say at 85 degrees. There it stops. The error is still 5 degrees, but that tiny error only makes a tiny push, and that tiny push is all it takes to balance gravity. So the arm holds at 85 degrees forever, 5 degrees short.

This chapter answers one question: how do we close that last little gap without cranking the gain up so high the arm starts to wobble?

The core idea: add up the error over time

The fix is to give the controller a memory. Instead of looking only at the error right now, it also keeps a running total of every error it has seen so far. That running total is the integral. "Integral" is just a fancy word for "the sum added up over time."

The whole idea: keep adding the leftover error into a running total. Even a small error, added again and again, grows into a total big enough to make a real push. So the controller keeps pushing a little harder every loop until the gap is actually gone.

Think about a cold room with a small steady draft. You nudge the thermostat up a bit. The room warms, but the draft keeps stealing a little heat, so the room settles just below the temperature you wanted. What do you do? You nudge it up a little more. And a little more. Each nudge adds to the last one. You keep nudging until the room finally holds the temperature you asked for. That "keep nudging until it holds" is exactly the integral term. The leftover miss keeps adding up until the total push is finally enough. You already do this every time you creep a thermostat up bit by bit.

Now put that on Sparky's arm. Proportional control alone settles at 85 degrees. The integral term notices the arm is still 5 degrees short, loop after loop. Every loop it adds that 5 degrees into its running total. The total grows, so the push grows, so the arm climbs. It climbs past 85, then 87, then 89, and the total keeps growing as long as any gap remains. Only when the arm reaches 90 degrees does the error hit zero. Then nothing more gets added, the total holds steady, and that held-up total supplies exactly the push needed to fight gravity at 90 degrees. The gap is gone.

Picture the error being added up

The best way to feel the integral is to picture the error as a height on a graph, and the running total as the shaded area underneath it. Every loop, a thin new slice of area gets added to the pile. The taller the error, the fatter the slice. But here is the key part: even when the error is small and flat, slices keep getting added, so the shaded area keeps growing. A small error that never goes away eventually builds a large total.

P plus I is PI

You do not throw away proportional control to use the integral. You add them together. The proportional part reacts fast to the error right now. The integral part slowly cleans up whatever the proportional part leaves behind. Run both at once and you have a PI controller.

  • P (the present): looks at the error right now and pushes in proportion to it. This does most of the fast work of getting close.
  • I (the past): looks at the running total of all past error and keeps pushing until that total has grown enough to close the last gap. This does the slow, patient cleanup.

Together they behave like a fast worker and a patient one. P sprints the arm most of the way. I quietly finishes the job so the arm lands exactly on 90 and stays.

Optional formula (skip it and lose nothing): \[ \text{correction} = \underbrace{K_p \times \text{error}}_{\text{present error}} \;+\; \underbrace{K_i \times (\text{running total of past error})}_{\text{added up over time}} \] The first piece is plain proportional control from Chapter 8. The second piece is the integral. \(K_i\) is a knob, like \(K_p\), that sets how strongly the running total pushes. Everything in this chapter is those two pieces added together.

The honest cost: windup

The integral is not free. Its whole trick is to keep adding error into a running total, and that same trick can backfire. Suppose the arm gets stuck on the way up. Maybe an artifact is jammed against it, or the arm is slow to move under a heavy load. The arm is not reaching the target, so the error stays big, and the integral keeps adding that big error into its total, loop after loop. The total balloons to a huge value.

Then the arm suddenly comes free. Now the controller is holding an enormous stored-up total, far more push than it needs. It slams the arm way past 90 degrees before that huge total finally drains back down. The arm overshoots, then has to come back. This build-up of too much stored total is called integral windup, or just windup.

So the integral has a personality: patient and thorough, but it can hold a grudge. It remembers every bit of leftover error, which is exactly what closes the gap, and also exactly what causes windup. Real FTC code guards against this by capping how large the running total is allowed to grow, so it cannot balloon while the robot is stuck. That cap is called an integral limit or anti-windup. You do not need the details yet. Just know the trade-off is real: I closes the gap, but left unguarded it can overshoot.

Look ahead: notice what the integral ends up doing on the arm. Once the arm holds at 90 degrees, that stored total is really just supplying a steady push to fight gravity. If you already know how much push gravity needs, why make the integral slowly discover it every time? That shortcut is the kG gravity term you will meet in Chapter 13. The same idea shows up on a vertical linear slide, where gravity pulls on the slide the whole way up.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's arm is commanded to 90 degrees. With proportional control only, the arm rises and then holds steady at 85 degrees, five degrees short, because gravity keeps pulling. The team switches on the integral term as well (so now it is PI). Describe what happens to the arm over the next second or two, and why.

What P alone was doing. At 85 degrees the error is \(90-85=5\) degrees. That five-degree error makes a small proportional push, and that small push exactly balances gravity. So the arm holds at 85 and never moves. The error is stuck at five degrees.

What the integral adds. The integral keeps a running total of the error. Every loop it adds the current five-degree error into that total. Loop after loop, the total grows: five, then ten, then fifteen, and so on. Because the total grows, the integral push grows, so the total push (P plus I) becomes bigger than gravity. The arm starts rising again.

Where it stops. The arm climbs past 85, and the error shrinks as it goes, but the total keeps growing as long as any gap remains. The arm reaches 90 degrees. Now the error is zero, so nothing more gets added to the total. The total holds at whatever value it reached, and that held total supplies exactly the push needed to hold the arm at 90 against gravity. The arm settles on the target.

Aha: the integral does not need the error to be large. A small error that refuses to go away is enough, because adding it up over time turns a small miss into a big enough push.

Example 2. Coach Brian watches Sparky's arm run with PI. An artifact jams the arm halfway up for a couple of seconds. When the artifact pops loose, the arm flies up well past 90 degrees before swinging back and settling. The driver asks, "We added I to fix the gap, so why is it overshooting now?" Explain what happened.

During the jam. The arm is stuck far below 90, so the error stays large the whole time. The integral keeps adding that large error into its running total every loop. Because the jam lasts a couple of seconds, the total grows to a huge value. Nothing tells it to stop, because from the controller's point of view the arm simply is not reaching the target.

When it frees. Now the arm can move, but the controller is holding a giant stored total. That total demands a giant push. The arm shoots up and blows past 90 degrees. Only after the arm has spent time above the target does the error go negative and start draining the total back down. Once the total drains, the arm falls back and settles on 90.

Aha: this is windup. It is not a new bug you created; it is the integral doing its one job (remembering leftover error) at the worst possible time. The fix is to cap how big the running total can grow, so a long jam cannot balloon it.
Common pitfalls:
  • Reaching for the integral to go faster. The integral is not for speed. It is slow and patient, made to clean up a leftover gap. If the robot is too sluggish, that is a proportional-gain question, not an integral one. Use I to kill a steady miss, not to sprint.
  • Forgetting windup exists. If the robot can get stuck, pinned, or held (a jammed artifact, an arm against a hard stop), the integral total can balloon and cause a big overshoot later. Always guard the integral with a limit when the mechanism can stall.
  • Setting the integral gain too high. A large \(K_i\) makes the total grow fast, which can overshoot the target and then oscillate slowly around it. Start the integral gain small and raise it just until the steady gap disappears.

12. PID Together

Three personalities working as one team. P watches the error right now, I remembers the leftover error from the past, and D predicts where things are heading next. Add the three pushes together and you get the controller FTC teams use most.

Prerequisites: Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD) · Chapter 11: Integral Control (PI)
This chapter: PID Together
Unlocks: Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF

You already type this into your code

Open your team's robot code and find where you set up a motor controller. Somewhere in there are three numbers with names like kP, kI, and kD. You have probably tuned them by trial and error, nudging one up, another down, until Sparky's arm or flywheel behaved. This chapter tells you what each of those three numbers actually does.

You have already met all three ideas on their own:

  • P is Chapter 8: Proportional Control. It pushes in proportion to how far off you are right now. It is the main worker.
  • D is the brake from Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD). It watches how fast the error is changing and eases off before you arrive, so you do not overshoot.
  • I is the memory from Chapter 11: Integral Control (PI). It adds up the leftover error over time and keeps pushing until even a tiny gap is gone.

PID is just all three, running at the same time, added together. That is the whole idea. The rest of this chapter is about why putting them together works so well.

The core idea: three personalities, one motor command

The cleanest way to hold PID in your head is to think of three people watching Sparky, each with a different job. Each one shouts out how hard to push. The controller listens to all three, adds up what they say, and sends that one number to the motor.

Here is what makes them a team. Each personality looks at a different slice of time.

  • P looks at the PRESENT. It asks "how far off am I right now?" If the flywheel is 400 RPM below target, P pushes hard. As the gap shrinks, P eases off on its own. P is the workhorse that does most of the moving.
  • I looks at the PAST. It asks "how much error has been piling up over time?" If Sparky keeps resting a little short of the target, I remembers that leftover gap and slowly grows its push until the gap is erased. I is the one that refuses to accept "close enough."
  • D looks at the near FUTURE. It asks "how fast is the error changing, and where will that put me in a moment?" If the flywheel is racing toward the target fast, D predicts it is about to blow past and pushes back to slow it down early. D is the brake.

Why they need each other

Here is the reason PID is a team and not just three copies of the same idea. Each personality covers a weakness the others have. You saw each weakness in its own chapter. Line them up and the fix becomes obvious.

  • P alone is fast but imperfect. It moves Sparky quickly, but from Chapter 8 you know two problems: crank it up and it overshoots and wobbles (Chapter 9), and against a constant pull like gravity it settles a little short and leaves a leftover gap.
  • D fixes P's wobble. The brake from Chapter 10 senses Sparky rushing at the target and eases off early, so you can use a strong P for speed without paying for it in overshoot. D tames the wobble that P causes.
  • I fixes P's leftover gap. The memory from Chapter 11 keeps adding up that small steady miss until its push grows large enough to close it. I removes the steady-state error that P alone cannot.
Aha: P is the workhorse that does most of the work. D is the brake that stops it from overshooting. I is the memory that erases the last little gap. Each one is weak somewhere, and exactly there another one is strong. That is why the three together beat any one alone.

The controller adds the three pushes together

All three personalities look at the same error at the same instant. Each turns that error into its own push. The controller simply adds the three pushes into one number and sends it to the motor. This block diagram shows the one error splitting into three terms and the three coming back together.

Optional formula (you can skip this and lose nothing): \[ \text{output} = \underbrace{(\text{P part})}_{\text{present}} + \underbrace{(\text{I part})}_{\text{past}} + \underbrace{(\text{D part})}_{\text{future}} \] That is the whole controller in one line. Three pushes, added. Each part is what you already learned: the P part is \(k_P \times \text{error}\), the I part grows from the piled-up error, and the D part comes from how fast the error is changing. You never add these by hand. The robot does it every loop, and your job is just to pick good values for \(k_P\), \(k_I\), and \(k_D\).

Read the curves as a story. P is fast but a little short. Adding D (PD) smooths the ride but the gap stays. Adding I instead (PI) closes the gap but brings back the wobble. Only when all three run together does the curve rise fast, stay calm, and settle right on the target. That green curve is what a well-tuned PID looks like, and it is why PID is the controller FTC teams reach for most.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's flywheel must hold 3000 RPM (revolutions per minute) so every shot flies the same distance. Theo runs it with P only. It spins up fast but settles at 2850 RPM and stays there, 150 RPM short. He adds an I term. Now it reaches 3000, but it overshoots to 3200 first and wobbles a bit before settling. Which single term should he add next, and what will it do?

Read what each behavior means. P only settling at 2850 is a leftover gap of 150 RPM. That is steady-state error, the constant miss P leaves against a load (here, the drag of the spinning wheel and the artifacts hitting it). Adding I fixed the gap, because I remembers the piled-up error and keeps pushing until it is gone. But the new overshoot to 3200 and the wobble are the classic sign of a push that is too strong near the target.

The fix. Add the D term to make it full PID. D watches how fast the speed is climbing. When the wheel is rushing toward 3000, D sees it is about to blow past and eases off early, which cuts the overshoot and calms the wobble. Now Theo has P doing the main spin-up, I erasing the leftover gap, and D braking so it does not overshoot. That is why full PID lands cleanly on 3000.

Aha: the term you add is chosen by the symptom. A leftover gap that never closes means "add I." Overshoot and wobble mean "add D." Slow to react means "more P." Each letter has one job.

Example 2. Coach Brian points at three phrases on the whiteboard: "how far off right now," "the gap that keeps piling up," and "how fast things are changing." The driver asks Theo to match each phrase to P, I, or D, and to say which slice of time each one watches.

Match them up.

  • "How far off right now" is P. It watches the present error and pushes in step with it.
  • "The gap that keeps piling up" is I. It watches the past, adding up the leftover error over time until its push grows big enough to erase it.
  • "How fast things are changing" is D. It watches the near future, using the speed of change to predict where the error is heading and braking before an overshoot.

Why this matters. Once you can name which slice of time a symptom lives in, you know which knob to turn. A miss that never goes away is a past problem, so it is an I job. A swing past the target is a future problem, so it is a D job. Being far off right now is a present problem, so it is a P job.

Aha: past, present, future is not just a memory trick. It is a decision rule. Match the symptom to a slice of time, and the slice of time tells you the term.

Example 3. Theo aims Sparky at the goal with full PID. Sparky has no turret, so aiming means rotating the whole robot until the camera under the shooter is centered on the goal. The heading comes from the IMU in degrees. He commands Sparky to turn from a heading of 0 degrees to 60 degrees. It rotates fast, brakes smoothly near 60 without overshooting, and holds exactly on 60. Describe, one at a time, what P, I, and D are each contributing during this turn.

Early in the turn (heading near 0, target 60). The error is a big 60 degrees. P sees that large present error and pushes hard, so the robot spins fast. I has barely started adding up error yet, so its push is still small. D sees the robot starting to rotate quickly and adds a little braking, but not much yet.

Approaching the target (heading near 55). The error is small now, so P eases way off on its own. D becomes important here: the robot is still turning fast toward 60, so D predicts an overshoot and pushes back to slow it, easing the robot onto 60 instead of past it.

Resting on target (heading at 60). The error is zero, so P contributes nothing. D contributes nothing, because the robot is not turning, so the error is not changing. If any tiny drift or floor scrub were nudging the robot off 60, I would notice the small error piling up and add just enough push to hold it exactly on 60.

Aha: the three terms take turns leading. P leads the fast part, D leads the braking near the end, and I quietly holds the final heading. You do not switch between them. They run all at once, and whichever one matters most at that moment naturally dominates.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking PID is a brand-new idea. It is not. It is only P, I, and D, the three terms you already learned, added together. If you understand each letter, you understand PID.
  • Turning up all three at once. When Sparky misbehaves, do not crank kP, kI, and kD together. Match the symptom to one term: leftover gap means I, overshoot and wobble means D, sluggish means more P. Change one thing at a time (that is the whole point of Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller).
  • Assuming you always need all three. Many FTC mechanisms run fine on just P or PD. You add I only when a leftover gap actually bothers you, and D only when overshoot actually bothers you. More terms is not automatically better.
  • Mixing up I and D. They sound similar but look at opposite ends of time. I looks backward at error that has piled up. D looks forward at how fast error is changing right now. Backward is memory, forward is prediction.

P2. Sparky's arm is commanded from 0 degrees to 45 degrees and must hold there against gravity. With P only, it rises fast but settles at 41 degrees and stays there, 4 degrees short. What is happening. The 4-degree leftover is steady-state error. It depends on the error piled up over time, so even a tiny remaining miss slowly builds a push big enough to hold 45 exactly. That is the one thing P structurally cannot do.

13. Feedforward and PIDF (kS, kV, kA, kG)

Work out the push the task will need before any error shows up, apply it up front, and let PID clean up the small leftover. This is why FTC flywheels and arms feel so snappy.

Prerequisites: Chapter 12: PID Together
This chapter: Feedforward and PIDF (kS, kV, kA, kG)
Unlocks: Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller · Chapter 16: Motion Profiling and Path Following

A problem your PID still has

Theo has done everything right. In Chapter 12: PID Together he tuned a full PID controller for Sparky's flywheel. The flywheel is the spinning wheel that flings each artifact. Its target is a speed, 3000 RPM (rotations per minute), so every shot flies the same distance. His PID holds 3000 nicely once it gets there.

But two things still bug him. First, when he spins the wheel up from a stop, it takes almost a full second to climb to 3000. During that second the wheel is not at speed, so he cannot shoot. Second, every time the driver feeds an artifact, the artifact slaps the wheel and drags it down toward 2600 RPM. The PID does claw it back to 3000, but it takes a moment, and any shot during that moment misses.

Coach Brian watches and asks the question that starts this chapter. "Your controller waits until the speed is wrong before it does anything. You already know it takes about this much power to spin at 3000. Why not just apply that power right away, instead of waiting for the wheel to fall behind?"

The core idea: predict the output, don't wait for the error

Here is the one thing that makes PID slow in those moments. PID only reacts after an error appears. Remember the error from Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop: error is the target minus the measurement, how far off you are right now. PID looks at that error and pushes. No error means no push. So PID has to let the wheel fall behind first, then catch up. It is always one step late.

The whole idea: feedforward figures out the output the task will need and applies it up front, before any error shows up. It predicts. Then feedback (your PID) only has to fix the small leftover. Feedforward does the bulk of the work early, so the error barely appears at all.

The name says it. Feed-forward sends the output forward to the motor ahead of time, based on what you already know the job needs. Feed-back waits for the measurement to come back and reacts to the miss. A good controller uses both.

This is the one fact to hold on to, because it is what makes feedforward different from everything before it:

Feedforward does NOT use the error. P, I, and D all start from the error (target minus measurement). Feedforward never looks at the error at all. It works out its push straight from the target and what you know about the machine. That is exactly why it can act before the error exists. If something computes a push from the error, it is feedback (PID), not feedforward.

Here is the whole controller as a picture. Follow the two paths. The green path (feedforward) reads the target and predicts a push. The blue path (feedback) makes the error and lets PID react to it. The two pushes add together at the plus junction and drive the motor. This block diagram was drawn with Graphviz, the same tool engineers use to sketch control loops.

The four feedforward pushes: kS, kV, kA, kG

Feedforward needs one thing: some knowledge of how the machine behaves, boiled down to a few numbers you find once and reuse. In FTC almost every feedforward is built from four simple pieces. Each has a name that is just the letter k (for "constant") plus one more letter, and each has a plain job. You will meet all four in the FTC control libraries.

  • kS, the get-moving push (Static friction). Every mechanism has some stickiness that must be broken before it moves at all. Static friction is the grip that holds a still part still. kS is a small, constant base push that overcomes it so the part starts moving instead of sitting there buzzing. Think of the first hard shove to get a heavy box sliding.
  • kV, the speed push (proportional to target Velocity). This is the star for a flywheel. To spin at a steady speed you need a steady amount of power, and roughly, twice the speed needs twice the power. kV is the number that turns a target speed into that power. To spin at 3000 RPM you mostly already know the push it takes, so feedforward applies that known power directly instead of making PID discover it. kV multiplies the target speed, not the error.
  • kA, the get-there-faster push (proportional to target Acceleration). When you want the speed or position to change (speed up or slow down), you need extra push on top of just holding steady. kA is the number that supplies that extra push while the target is speeding up or slowing down. The harder you want to accelerate, the more kA adds. Once you reach the target and stop changing, the kA push drops back to zero, because there is no acceleration left to help with. kA multiplies the target acceleration, not the error.
  • kG, the hold-it-up push (Gravity). An arm or a vertical slide is pulled down by gravity the whole time, even when it is sitting exactly where you want it. kG is a constant push that just holds the weight up. With kG carrying the weight, PID no longer has to fight gravity, so it can sit right on the target instead of a few degrees or inches short. (For an arm, the pull is strongest when it sticks straight out level, so that is the hardest spot to hold.)
About that garbled name you may have seen. Some people write the whole controller as "PIDF" and then list the feedforward letters after it, which can come out looking like "PIDF SVA" or even a scrambled "PIDSVG." Do not let that confuse you. There are only two ideas here. The feedforward gain letters are kS, kV, kA, and kG (get-moving, speed, acceleration, gravity). And PIDF just means PID with a feedforward term added on top. That is all "the F" ever means: feedforward.
Aha: feedforward is you telling the robot what you already know. "It takes about this much power to spin at 3000." "It takes about this much to hold the arm up." "It takes this much extra to speed the wheel up quickly." You knew it before the match started, so there is no reason to make the controller rediscover it every time by falling behind first.

The everyday version: leaning into a hill

You already do feedforward on a bike. Imagine you are riding on flat ground and you see a hill coming. The reacting-only way (pure feedback) is to keep pedaling the same, wait until the hill slows you down, feel yourself slowing, and only then pedal harder. You lurch and lose speed first.

What you actually do is lean in and pedal harder as you reach the hill, before it slows you at all. You can see the hill, so you know the extra effort it will take, and you apply that effort up front. You barely slow down. That is feedforward: acting on what you know is coming, not waiting for the mistake. A little fine adjustment while climbing (a bit harder, a bit easier) is the feedback part cleaning up.

Optional math aside (you can skip this and lose nothing)

Optional formulas. For the flywheel, the feedforward push is mostly the get-moving term plus the speed term, with the acceleration term added only while it is spinning up: \[ \text{feedforward} = k_S + k_V \times \text{target speed} + k_A \times \text{target acceleration} \] For an arm holding still, the feedforward push is the constant hold-it-up term: \[ \text{feedforward} = k_G \] Then the full controller just adds feedback on top: \[ \text{output} = \underbrace{\text{feedforward}}_{\text{predict from target}} + \underbrace{\text{PID(error)}}_{\text{react to leftover}} \] Notice the feedforward part has no "error" in it anywhere. It is built only from the target speed and target acceleration. That is the whole point.

The second picture is the arm, where kG is the star. Sparky has an arm that must hold level to line up a shot. Level is the worst case, because that is where gravity pulls hardest. With plain P control the arm settles a few degrees low, because a small leftover error is the only thing making a push, and that push is all that holds the weight (this is the steady-state error from Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P)). Feedforward fixes it directly: kG supplies a constant push that just carries the arm's weight, so PID is free to sit right on the target. The exact same idea holds up a vertical slide (a lift that raises straight up), which is the version you will see in seasons other than DECODE.

Putting it together: PIDF

That is the whole trick. PIDF is just PID with a feedforward term added. The F on the end stands for feedforward. Every loop, the controller does two jobs and adds them:

  • Feedforward (the F): predict the push from the target (kS, kV, kA, and kG as the job needs). This runs even when the error is zero.
  • Feedback (the PID): take the leftover error and correct it, exactly as in Chapter 12: PID Together.

Feedforward gets you almost all the way there instantly, without waiting for a mistake. PID mops up the small difference between "what I predicted" and "what really happened," which is where the real world (a slightly worn wheel, an artifact, an uneven floor) sneaks in. This is why FTC flywheels and arms feel so much snappier with F. The feedforward removes the slow "let the error build up first" step that plain PID cannot avoid, so the mechanism reaches its target fast and holds it steadily.

Which terms go with which mechanism? You almost never use all four at once. A flywheel holds a speed, so it uses kS + kV (and kA while spinning up). A vertical slide or arm fights gravity, so it uses kG (often with kS). A horizontal slide does not fight gravity, so it uses kS but no kG. Name the load the mechanism fights first, then pick the terms that match it.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's flywheel must hold 3000 RPM. Theo runs plain PID and the wheel takes almost a second to spin up. His mentor says "add a kV feedforward term." In plain words, what does kV do here, and why does the wheel now reach 3000 much faster? Does kV look at the error?

What kV does. kV turns a target speed into the motor push that speed needs. Roughly, twice the speed needs about twice the push. So for a 3000 RPM target, kV works out "about this much power spins the wheel at 3000" and applies that power the instant the target is set.

Why it is faster. Plain PID starts with the wheel at rest, sees a big error, and builds its push up from that error over time. That build-up is the slow second. kV skips the build-up. It hands the wheel almost the exact holding power right away, so the wheel jumps to near 3000 immediately and PID only trims the last little bit.

Does kV use the error? No. kV multiplies the target speed (3000), not the error. That is the whole reason it can act before the wheel has fallen behind. If it used the error, it would be just another feedback term, no faster than PID.

Aha: kV is you telling the robot the answer you already knew: "it takes about this much power to spin at 3000." There is no reason to make PID rediscover it slowly every match.

Example 2. Sparky has an arm that must hold level to line up a shot. With proportional control it always settles about 5 degrees below level and stays there. A teammate wants to crank the P gain way up to force it level. Coach Brian says to add a kG feedforward term instead. Why is kG the better fix?

Why P settles low. Gravity pulls the arm down the whole time. As the arm nears level the P push shrinks with the error. It stops shrinking at the angle where the small push just balances gravity, about 5 degrees low. The leftover 5-degree error is the only thing making a push, and that push is exactly what holds the arm up, so it sits there. This is steady-state error from Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P).

Why cranking P is a bad fix. A higher gain makes the same error produce a bigger push, so the arm settles a bit closer, maybe 2 degrees low. But it never fully closes the gap, because zero error means zero push and zero push cannot hold the weight. Worse, high gain brings back overshoot and oscillation (Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation), so the arm swings past level and wobbles.

Why kG works. kG supplies a constant push that carries the arm's weight all by itself, without needing any error to make it. Once gravity is held, the arm no longer needs a leftover error to stay up, so PID can settle right on level. The gap closes because the thing that caused it (a constant pull with nothing to balance it) is now balanced by feedforward.

Pattern: feedforward beats a constant, known load; feedback beats the surprises. Gravity on an arm and drag on a flywheel are constant and known ahead of time, so feedforward (kG, kV) is the right tool. An artifact hit, a bumped robot, or a worn wheel are surprises, so feedback (PID) handles those. Reach for feedforward whenever you can predict the push before the error appears.

Example 3. Two flywheel controllers both aim for 3000 RPM. Controller A is plain PID. Controller B is PIDF with a kV term. The driver feeds an artifact into each. Controller A dips to 2600 RPM and takes a moment to recover. Controller B dips only to about 2850 and snaps back almost at once. Explain the difference, and be careful to say what the feedforward is and is not doing.

What is the same. When the artifact hits, both wheels slow down, so both show a dip. The artifact is a surprise (a disturbance), and only feedback reacts to a surprise, because feedforward does not watch the wheel.

What the feedforward is doing. The kV term keeps supplying the full holding power for 3000 RPM the entire time, because it is based on the target, which never changed. So even during the dip the wheel is still being driven hard by feedforward. On the plain PID wheel, the holding power came from the controller slowly building up its push against the error, and the artifact knocks that build-up down, so it has to slowly build it back. The PIDF wheel never lost its baseline push, so its PID only has to patch the small remaining dip.

What the feedforward is NOT doing. Feedforward does not sense the artifact and does not shrink the dip by reacting to it. It cannot, because it never looks at the error. The faster recovery comes from feedforward carrying the steady load so feedback is free to handle just the transient.

Aha: feedforward and feedback are a team with a clean split. Feedforward handles what you can predict (the steady push to hold 3000). Feedback handles what you cannot (the exact moment an artifact hits). Neither does the other's job.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking feedforward reacts to the error. It does not, ever. Feedforward computes its push from the target and what you know about the machine. If a term uses the error, it is P, I, or D, not feedforward. This is the single most important thing to get right about the F.
  • Thinking feedforward replaces PID. It does not. Feedforward is a prediction, and predictions are never perfect (the wheel wears, the battery sags, an artifact hits). PID is still there to fix the leftover. PIDF is both together, not one instead of the other.
  • Using the wrong term for the job. kV is for speed targets like the flywheel. kA adds push while that speed is changing. kG is for holding a weight like an arm or vertical slide. kS is the small base push to break static friction and get moving. Grabbing kG for a flywheel or kV for an arm hold does not match the job.
  • Expecting feedforward to fix a surprise. An artifact hit, a bump, or an uneven tile are disturbances feedforward cannot predict, so it will not react to them. Those are feedback's job. Feedforward only handles the part you know in advance.

P1. Sparky's flywheel is set to hold 3000 RPM. It has a kV feedforward term and a full PID. At one instant the wheel is spinning at exactly 3000 RPM, so the error is zero. P2. Sparky has a vertical slide that raises a scoring mechanism straight up. Gravity pulls it down the whole time, even when it is parked at the right height. Does it use the error? No. kG is a constant push set from what you know about the weight, not from the error. That is why it can hold the slide up even at the exact moment the error is zero.

14. Tuning a Controller

You picked a controller. Now you have to set its numbers. The one rule that saves you: more power is not always better. Learn to read what your robot is doing, then turn the right knob.

Prerequisites: Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF
This chapter: Tuning a Controller
Unlocks: Chapter 15: Take-Back-Half · points forward to Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism

You have now met the whole controller family. Bang-bang, proportional (P), the derivative brake (PD), the integral term (PI), all three together as PID, and feedforward (PIDF). Each one has a job. But every one of them has numbers you must set, and this is the part beginners get wrong the most.

Those numbers are called gains. A gain decides how strongly a term reacts. Tuning is the job of finding good values for the gains. This chapter is only about tuning: given a controller, how do you set its numbers so the robot behaves? Which controller to use for each mechanism is a different question, and that is Chapter 18. Here we assume you already picked one, and we make it work.

The one rule: more gain is NOT always better

The instinct every beginner has is "if it is not working, turn it up." That instinct is a trap. You saw why back in Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation. Turn the P gain too low and the robot is sluggish and slow. Turn it too high and it overshoots and wobbles. The good value is in the middle.

This is true for every gain, not just P. Each one has a "too little," a "just right," and a "too much." And the too-much end is its own kind of broken. Tuning is not "crank everything to the max." Tuning is a search for the middle.

Aha: a gain that is too high is not "extra safe." It is a different failure. Every knob has a too-much end, so half the time the right move is to turn a knob down.

A safe order to tune in

Do not turn all the knobs at once. If you change three numbers and the robot gets worse, you have no idea which one did it. Change one thing at a time, watch what the robot does, then change the next. Here is a safe order that works for most FTC mechanisms. It goes from the biggest, most obvious effect down to the smallest cleanup.

  1. Set feedforward first, if you are using it. For a flywheel or a gravity-loaded arm, set the feedforward before anything else (the kV, kG, kS terms from Chapter 13). Get the predicted push close so the mechanism almost holds the target on its own. Now PID only has a small leftover to fix.
  2. Raise P until it responds well, but stop before it wobbles. Turn the P gain up from a low value. The robot gets quicker and quicker. The moment it starts to overshoot and buzz around the target, you have gone too far. Back it off a little. This is the biggest knob, so get it roughly right first.
  3. Add D to kill the overshoot. If the mechanism still swings past the target, add a little D. The D term is the brake: it senses you are arriving fast and eases off early. Add just enough to stop the overshoot. Too much D makes the robot jittery, more on that below.
  4. Add a little I to erase the leftover gap. If the robot settles close to the target but a small steady gap remains (from gravity, friction, a dragging cable), add a small I. The I term remembers that gap and slowly pushes it out. Use a little; too much I causes its own overshoot.
Aha: the order is not random. You tune the term with the biggest effect first, so each later, smaller knob is adjusting a robot that is already almost right. Tune P before D before I, and always one at a time.

The symptom map: read the robot, then fix the right knob

This is the most useful thing in the chapter. When Sparky misbehaves, you do not guess. You watch what it does, match that behavior to a row in this table, and turn the exact knob the table names. Learn to read the behavior first, then reach for the knob.

What you SEE (symptom) What is wrong (cause) What to do (fix)
Slow and lazy. Takes forever to reach the target. Too little P. Raise P.
Overshoots and wobbles back and forth around the target. Too much P. Lower P, or add some D to brake it.
Reaches the target but keeps overshooting, with a slow, delayed, laggy feel. Too much I (windup). Lower I.
Jittery, twitchy, noisy. The motor buzzes even when the robot is near still. Too much D (it amplifies sensor noise). Lower D.
Settles calmly but a little short of the target, and stays there. Leftover steady-state gap (a constant load pushes back). Add a little I, or add feedforward to predict the load.

Notice the shape of the table. Every knob has a "too much" failure, not just a "too little" one. Too much P wobbles. Too much I overshoots and lags. Too much D gets jittery. That is the whole "more is not better" lesson in one column. When something is wrong, the fix is often to turn a knob down, not up.

Two of the rows can look alike, so pay attention to the timing. Too much P overshoots right away and wobbles fast. Too much I overshoots with a slow, delayed, laggy feel, because it takes time for the summed-up error to build and then unwind. Same overshoot, different speed. Watch how quickly it wobbles to tell them apart.

Worked examples

Example 1. Theo tunes the PD controller that rotates Sparky to face the goal (target heading 90 degrees). He turns P up and up to make it fast. Now the robot races to 90, swings past to about 105, comes back to 80, up to 98, and wobbles for a full second before holding. Using the symptom map, name the cause and the fix, and explain why "turn P up more" would be the wrong move.

Match the symptom. The robot overshoots the target and wobbles back and forth around it. That is the "too much P" row of the symptom map.

The cause. With P set high, the controller is still shoving hard even when the robot is almost at 90. The robot is already spinning fast, so its momentum plus the hard shove carry it past the target. Once past, the error flips sign and it shoves back the other way, over and over.

The fix. The table gives two options: lower P, or add some D. Since Theo wants to keep the fast response, the better move is to add D. The D brake senses the robot is closing in fast and eases the push off early, so it arrives without swinging past. If the wobble is severe, back P down a little first, then add D.

Why "more P" is wrong. The robot is already wobbling because P is too strong. Turning it up makes the shove even harder, so it overshoots even more and wobbles longer. More gain is not better. When you see overshoot and wobble, the knob goes down or the brake goes on, never up.

Example 2. Sparky's linear slide raises the scoring mechanism and must stop at exactly the right height, but gravity always pulls it down. Theo runs it with plain P. It rises fast and stops smoothly, but it always settles about an inch below the target and just sits there, an inch short, forever. He also notices the motor is quiet and steady, no buzzing. Name the symptom, the cause, and two possible fixes.

Match the symptom. It settles calmly but a little short of the target and stays there. No wobble, no jitter. That is the bottom row of the symptom map, the leftover steady-state gap.

The cause. Gravity is a constant load. As the slide nears the target the proportional push shrinks, and it stops at the height where that shrinking push exactly balances gravity. The one-inch error still exists, but it only makes a tiny push, and that tiny push is all it takes to hold against gravity. So the slide hangs there. P alone cannot close the gap, because a zero error would mean zero push, and zero push cannot hold weight.

Two fixes.

  • Add a little I. The integral term remembers the leftover inch and keeps adding push until the gap is gone. Use a small value so it does not overshoot.
  • Add feedforward (the kG term). Feedforward predicts the steady push needed to hold the slide's weight and applies it up front, before any error builds up. This is cleaner for a gravity load because it fixes the cause instead of waiting for the gap to appear. A vertical slide is a PIDF job, from Chapter 13.
Aha: a calm, steady miss is the fingerprint of a steady load. The fix is the I term or feedforward, never more P. More P just settles a hair closer while risking wobble, and it never truly closes the gap.
Common pitfalls:
  • Turning everything up when something is wrong. The instinct "it is not working, crank it" causes more problems than it fixes. Every gain has a too-much failure. Read the symptom first, and be ready to turn a knob down.
  • Changing several gains at once. If you change P, I, and D together and the robot changes, you cannot tell which knob did it. Change one, watch, then change the next.
  • Tuning out of order. If you add I or D before P is roughly right, you are cleaning up a robot that is still badly off, and the small knobs cannot save a bad P. Get P close first, then D, then I. Feedforward before all of them.
  • Confusing "too much I" with "too much P." Both can cause overshoot. Too much P overshoots right away and wobbles fast. Too much I overshoots with a slow, delayed, laggy feel. Watch the timing to tell them apart.
  • Blaming a gravity-load gap on bad tuning. A calm settle a little short is not a bug and not too little P. It is steady-state error from a constant load. The cure is I or feedforward, not more P.

★ Landmarks it points toward

  • Ziegler-Nichols tuning: a classic recipe engineers use to pick starting gains. You raise P until the robot oscillates steadily, note that gain and the wobble's timing, and plug them into a formula. It gives a fast first guess you then fine-tune by hand.

  • Gain scheduling: using different gains in different situations (for example, a lighter push when the arm is empty and a stronger one when it carries an artifact). One fixed set of gains is not always best across every load.

Practice

P1. Match each symptom to its cause and the knob to change. (a) Sparky's arm reaches its target but the motor buzzes and twitches the whole time it holds. (b) Sparky's drivetrain creeps toward its target so slowly the autonomous period runs out. (c) Sparky's rotation to face the goal overshoots and wobbles several times before it holds.

Show answer

Read each symptom against the symptom map.

  • (a) Buzzing and twitching while holding = too much D. The derivative term reacts to how fast the error changes, and it amplifies the small random jitter in the sensor. Lower D.
  • (b) Too slow, never gets there in time = too little P. The push is too weak for the distance. Raise P.
  • (c) Overshoot and wobble = too much P. Lower P, or add some D to brake the arrival.

Name the behavior before you touch a knob: each of these is a different knob, and you could not tell which without first reading what the robot actually does. Buzzing points to D, sluggishness points to P, and wobble points to P. Read, then turn.

P2. Coach Brian says, "Our flywheel controller settles at 2950 RPM when we ask for 3000, every single match. It is calm, no wobble, no buzzing, it just sits at 2950." Theo says, "Easy, I will turn P way up until it hits 3000." Is Theo right? If not, what is the real cause and the real fix?

Show answer

Theo is not right. A calm, steady miss with no wobble is the fingerprint of a leftover steady-state gap, the bottom row of the symptom map. It is caused by a constant load, here the drag on the wheel, not by too little P.

Why more P is the wrong fix. Turning P up would settle the wheel a little closer, maybe 2975, but it can never fully reach 3000, because a zero error would mean zero correction and zero correction cannot overcome the constant drag. Worse, a high P gain brings on overshoot and wobble, trading a calm 50 RPM miss for an unsteady speed that wrecks shot consistency.

The real fix. Add a little I, or lean on the feedforward. The I term remembers the 50 RPM gap and keeps nudging the power up until the wheel truly reaches 3000. On a flywheel, well-tuned feedforward (the kV term) predicts most of the needed power up front, so the gap barely appears in the first place. Both fix the cause, a steady load, instead of fighting it with raw P.

Pattern, a calm miss means I or feedforward, never more P: whenever the robot settles steadily a little short of a target under a constant load, reach for the integral term or feedforward. Save P increases for when the robot is too slow, and be ready to turn P down when it wobbles.

P3. Theo sits down to tune a fresh PIDF controller for Sparky's flywheel. All four gains start at zero. He is tempted to set P, I, and D to reasonable-looking numbers all at once and see what happens. Give him the safe order to set the gains, and one sentence on why that order.

Show answer

The order: feedforward first (the kV term for a flywheel), then raise P until the wheel responds but before it wobbles, then add D only if it overshoots, then a little I to erase any leftover gap. One knob at a time, watching the wheel after each change.

Why: feedforward does most of the work so PID has only a small leftover, and tuning from the biggest effect (P) down to the smallest cleanup (I) means every later knob is adjusting a wheel that is already almost right. Setting all four at once means that if it misbehaves, he has no idea which number to blame.

15. Take-Back-Half

A tiny velocity controller made for flywheels. It keeps pushing until the speed crosses the target, then snaps its own output halfway back. That one clever trick lets it settle on a steady speed with basically one number to tune and no derivative.

Prerequisites: Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller
This chapter: Take-Back-Half (TBH)
Unlocks: Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism · returns in Chapter 19: Payoff, the Flywheel Shooter

You just spent a whole chapter learning to tune a PID controller: nudge one gain, watch the response, nudge another, repeat until it behaves. It works, but it is fiddly, especially for a flywheel where a bad guess can leave the speed wobbling for a long time. This chapter shows a different controller built for exactly that job. It is called Take-Back-Half, or TBH for short. It settles a flywheel onto a steady speed with essentially one number to tune and no derivative term at all. If your team has ever grabbed a "TBH flywheel" snippet from a VEX or FTC forum and it just worked, you have used this without knowing its name.

Where this fits: TBH is an alternative to velocity PID, not a part of PID. It is its own little controller. You would use TBH instead of a PID/PIDF loop on a flywheel, not on top of one. Everything you learned about error still applies, but the way TBH uses that error is its own trick.

The problem TBH is built for

Remember Sparky's flywheel. It is the spinning wheel that flings 5-inch artifacts into the goal, and every shot only flies the same distance if the wheel holds the same speed. The target is 3000 RPM. RPM means rotations per minute, a measure of how fast the wheel spins. The motor encoder reads the real speed many times a second.

A flywheel is a hard thing to hold steady. It is heavy and spins fast, so it takes real power to speed it up, and it coasts for a while when you cut power. Bang-bang (Chapter 6) can hold it, but it ripples forever. A velocity PID or PIDF (Chapters 12 and 13) can hold it beautifully, but you have to tune it, and a flywheel is slow to test because each spin-up takes a few seconds. Theo wants something in between: smarter than bang-bang, but far less fiddly than tuning three or four PID gains. That is exactly the gap TBH fills.

The core idea, in plain words

TBH works in two moves. The first move is the same as the integral term you met in Chapter 11.

Move 1: keep adding up the error. Every loop, TBH looks at the error (target minus measured speed) and adds a little of it to the motor output. If the wheel is below 3000, the error is positive, so the output keeps climbing and the wheel keeps speeding up. This is just like the integral term: it piles up error over time and slowly builds the push. On its own, this would overshoot badly, because the output keeps growing right up until the moment the wheel finally reaches the target, so by then the output is way too high and the wheel blows past 3000.

Move 2: the clever part, take back half. Here is what makes it TBH. The moment the measured speed crosses the target, that is, the error flips from positive to negative (or back), the controller does something surprising. It jumps its output halfway back toward the value the output had at the previous crossing. It literally averages "the output right now" with "the output at the last time we crossed." That halving is where the name comes from: at every crossing, it takes back half of the change it just made.

Aha: the integral part is what causes the overshoot, because the output only stops growing after the speed is already past the target. The "take back half" jump is the fix: instead of letting the next swing be just as big, it chops the output halfway on every crossing, so each swing is smaller than the last. The wobble shrinks and shrinks until the speed just sits on 3000.

Why halfway, and not all the way or a little bit? If it took back everything, it would land on a guess and probably be wrong again. If it took back just a sliver, it would barely help and keep overshooting. Halfway is the sweet spot: it is a smart average that quickly homes in on the one output level that holds the target. Each crossing cuts the error in half, so after just a few crossings the speed is basically locked on.

Figure 1: Sparky's flywheel settling under TBH

Watch the speed over time. It spins up, overshoots 3000, and crosses the target. At that first crossing the output takes back half, so the next swing down is smaller. It crosses again, takes back half again, and the swing after that is smaller still. In a few crossings the saw-tooth has shrunk to nothing and the wheel is holding 3000.

Figure 2: what the output is doing

Figure 1 showed the speed. To really get TBH, look at what the motor output is doing underneath. While the speed is below target, the output ramps up steadily (that is Move 1, adding up the error). Then the speed crosses the target, and the output does not just keep going: it snaps halfway back toward its value at the last crossing (that is Move 2). It ramps again, snaps again, and each snap lands closer to one steady level, the exact output that holds 3000 RPM.

Aha: the whole job of a flywheel controller is to find one number: the exact motor output that holds 3000 RPM. TBH finds it by bracketing. Each crossing gives it a value that is a little too high and a little too low, and taking the halfway point squeezes those two bounds together until it lands on the right one.

One number to tune

TBH has basically one knob, usually called the gain. It controls how fast the output ramps up between crossings (how much of the error you add each loop). That is it. There is no separate P, I, and D to balance against each other, and there is no derivative term at all, so there is nothing that amplifies sensor noise (recall from Chapter 10 that the derivative term is the noisy one). Set the gain a bit high and it reaches the target faster but with a bigger first overshoot. Set it lower and it is gentler but slower. Because the "take back half" trick does the settling for you, even a rough gain still converges. That forgiving, one-knob feel is the reason TBH is so beloved in VEX and FTC: you can get a flywheel holding steady without a long tuning session.

Figure 3: TBH versus bang-bang, side by side

This is the payoff picture. Put TBH and bang-bang on the same axes, both aiming for 3000 RPM. Bang-bang keeps its saw-tooth ripple going forever, because it only has "full on" and "off" and can never hold still. TBH swings a few times, shrinks, and settles flat on the target. Same goal, very different ending.

Honest notes: where TBH fits and where it does not

  • It is really a velocity controller. TBH is built for holding a speed, like a flywheel or a spinning roller or intake wheel. It leans on the fact that a speed naturally crosses its target as it settles. It is not the tool for holding a position, like an arm angle or a linear slide height. For those, reach for PID with feedforward (Chapters 12 and 13), not TBH.
  • It is an alternative to velocity PID, not an upgrade to it. You pick one or the other for the flywheel. TBH trades a little peak performance for a lot less tuning. A carefully tuned PIDF can hold a flywheel just as well or better and recover from a shot a bit more crisply, but it costs you the tuning time.
  • No derivative means no noise amplification. Because TBH has no D term, the sensor noise that plagues a derivative controller is simply not a problem here. That is a quiet, real advantage.
  • Beloved because it is simple and self-settling. The reason you see TBH all over VEX and FTC flywheel code is not that it is theoretically the best. It is that it is short, forgiving, and settles itself with one knob. For a team that wants a steady shooter without a long tuning session, that is a great deal.

Practice

P1. Sparky's flywheel is spinning up under TBH toward 3000 RPM. The encoder shows the speed climbing: 2400, 2700, 2950, then 3050 RPM. At which reading does the controller "take back half," and what does that mean it does to the motor output?

Answer

The speed crosses the target between 2950 (still below 3000) and 3050 (now above 3000). So it is at the 3050 reading that the error flips sign, and that is when TBH takes back half. At that crossing the controller snaps its motor output halfway back toward the output value it had at the previous crossing, instead of letting the output keep climbing. That halving is what makes the next swing smaller than this one.

P2. Theo has TBH working nicely on the flywheel. Coach Brian asks him to also use TBH to hold Sparky's arm at a fixed angle (a position, held with a motor encoder). Is that a good idea? Why or why not?

Answer

No. TBH is a velocity controller, built for holding a speed like a flywheel. Holding an arm at a fixed angle is a position job, and it also fights gravity, which needs a kG feedforward term (from Chapter 13). TBH has no way to add that steady holding push and is not designed for position at all. The right tool for the arm is PID plus feedforward, not TBH.

P3. The driver is choosing between bang-bang and TBH for the flywheel, since both are simple. In one or two sentences, what does TBH give the driver that bang-bang cannot, and what is the cost?

Answer

TBH lets the flywheel settle on a steady 3000 RPM, so every shot flies the same distance, while bang-bang ripples around 3000 forever and never holds still. The cost is that TBH has one number to tune (its gain) and takes a few swings to settle, whereas bang-bang has nothing to tune and reacts instantly. For a shooter that needs consistency, TBH's steady speed is usually worth that small price.

Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking TBH is part of PID. It is not. It is a separate, standalone controller you use instead of velocity PID on a flywheel. Do not try to bolt it onto a PID loop.
  • Using TBH for position. TBH is for holding a speed, not an angle or a slide height. Positions want PID plus feedforward.
  • Expecting a perfectly instant recovery from a shot. When an artifact drags the wheel down, TBH does recover, but it settles by its usual shrinking-swings method. A well-tuned PIDF can snap back a touch crisper. TBH's win is simplicity, not peak performance.
  • Forgetting it still needs a good speed sensor. TBH is a closed loop. It only works because the motor encoder tells it the real speed so it can spot the crossings. Bad speed readings, no TBH.

16. Motion Profiling and Path Following (Pedro Pathing)

Do not slam toward a far spot and brake hard at the end. Plan a smooth trip first, then follow the plan. This is exactly what Pedro Pathing does for Sparky.

Prerequisites: Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF · Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller
This chapter: Motion Profiling and Path Following (Pedro Pathing)
Unlocks: Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism · used in Chapter 21: Autonomous Driving

A problem your PID still has

It is autonomous. Sparky needs to drive from the start line to a scoring spot about six feet away, then shoot. Theo already has a PID that drives to a target position, using the two-wheel odometry from Chapter 5: Sensors to know where the robot is on the field. So he just sets the target to the scoring spot and lets the PID go.

The robot lurches. At the start the error is huge (six feet), so PID sends nearly full power and Sparky rockets forward. Near the spot the robot is still moving fast, so it blows past the target, then has to reverse and creep back. It wobbles into place. It works, but it is slow and ugly, and every extra second in autonomous is a shot Sparky did not take.

Coach Brian watches and asks the question that starts this chapter. "You are pointing the robot at a spot six feet away and flooring it. Of course it overshoots. Why not decide ahead of time exactly how fast the robot should be going at every moment of the trip, and just follow that?"

The core idea: plan the trip, then follow it

Here is the whole shift in thinking. Do not aim straight at the faraway target and let the error decide your speed. Instead, before the robot moves at all, draw up a plan: a smooth speed for every instant of the trip. Speed up gently, cruise, then slow down before you arrive, so you glide onto the spot instead of crashing into it.

The whole idea: a motion profile is that plan. It is a schedule of how fast the robot should be moving at each moment: ramp the speed up, cruise at a steady speed, then ramp the speed back down so it reaches zero right as the robot lands on the target. Plotted as speed over time, that shape is a trapezoid.

Look at the plan drawn out. The height of the shape is speed. Reading left to right is time. There are three parts: a ramp up, a flat cruise, and a ramp down. And here is the neat part: the area filled in underneath the shape is the distance the robot covers. Speed for a while adds up to distance, so a taller or wider trapezoid means a longer trip.

Slam and brake vs. chasing a moving target

Why does following a plan beat the plain PID from the start of the chapter? Because the plan turns one impossible job into a stream of easy ones.

Plain PID stares at a target six feet away the whole time. The error is enormous, so it floors the motors, so it arrives fast and overshoots. With a motion profile, the target is never far away. At each instant the plan says "right now you should be here, moving this fast," and that spot is only a hair ahead of where the robot already is. The robot is always chasing a close, gently moving target. Small error, small correction, smooth ride.

How the plan gets followed: feedforward drives, feedback trims

Here is where Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF pays off. A motion profile is the perfect partner for feedforward, because the plan tells you the target speed and target acceleration at every instant, which is exactly what feedforward needs.

  • Feedforward does most of the driving. During the cruise, the plan says "go this fast," and kV (the speed push) supplies the power for that speed straight away. During the ramps, the plan says "speed up now" or "slow down now," and kA (the acceleration push) supplies the extra shove to change speed. Feedforward reads the plan, not the error, so it drives the robot along the plan without waiting for a mistake.
  • Feedback cleans up the little stuff. The plan is a prediction, and the real world nudges the robot off it a bit: a slick tile, a slightly worn wheel, a bump. A small PID watches where the robot actually is (from odometry), compares it to where the plan says it should be right now, and trims that little error. Because feedforward already did the heavy lifting, the error PID has to fix is tiny.

Notice this is the same PIDF picture from Chapter 13, just with a moving target instead of a fixed one. The plan feeds both paths: it drives feedforward, and it sets the moving spot that PID corrects toward. This block diagram was drawn with Graphviz, the same tool engineers use to sketch control loops.

Feedforward leads, feedback follows. A motion profile is not error-led. The plan and feedforward do the driving; PID only patches the small leftover. If you drop the profile and let a big raw error drive the motors, you are back to slam-and-brake. The plan exists so the error never gets big.

From one straight move to a whole curved path: Pedro Pathing

So far the plan was for one straight trip. But in autonomous Sparky rarely goes in a straight line. It needs to curve around the field, dodge the obelisk, and arrive at the scoring spot already facing the goal. Path following is the same idea stretched over a curved route: instead of a plan for one distance, you plan a whole smooth path across the field, and the robot tracks it.

The path is a curved line drawn on the field from the start to the goal. Along that line the robot still has a motion profile (ramp up, cruise, ramp down), and at every instant the plan says where on the curve the robot should be, how fast, and which way to face. Feedforward drives it along the curve; PID trims it back onto the line whenever it drifts off. Because Sparky has a mecanum drivetrain, it can strafe and rotate while it drives, so it can follow curves and even spin to face the goal on the way.

This is what Pedro Pathing does for you. You do not write the profile math yourself. In FTC, the Pedro Pathing library takes the curved path you draw, builds the motion profile along it, and runs the feedforward-plus-PID follower on Sparky's mecanum drivetrain, using two-wheel odometry to know where the robot is. You hand it a path and a destination; it handles the smooth trip. Everything in this chapter is the theory of what that library is doing under the hood.

Worked examples

Example 1. Theo drives Sparky to a scoring spot with a plain position PID and it overshoots and wobbles every time. He switches to a motion profile for the same move. Explain, in plain words, why the profile stops the overshoot. Does the robot ever go slower on purpose?

Why the overshoot happens. Plain PID looks at the whole distance to the target as one big error. Far away, that error is huge, so it commands nearly full power and the robot is still moving fast when it reaches the spot. Fast robot plus "you are here" equals sailing past it.

Why the profile fixes it. The profile plans a ramp-down before the target. It tells the robot to start slowing early so its planned speed is basically zero right as it lands on the spot. The robot arrives already slow, so there is nothing to overshoot.

Does it slow down on purpose? Yes, and that is the point. It deliberately eases off during the ramp-down instead of braking hard at the end. Slowing early on purpose is what makes the arrival gentle.

Aha: plain PID brakes at the target; a motion profile brakes before it. Braking before is what lands the robot softly.

Example 2. During a profiled move, name what the kV term and the kA term are each doing (a) during the flat cruise in the middle, and (b) during the ramp-down at the end. Remember kV is the speed push and kA is the acceleration push, both from Chapter 13.

(a) During the cruise. The plan holds a steady speed, so kV supplies the steady push that keeps that speed, exactly like it holds a flywheel at 3000 RPM. There is no change in speed during the cruise, so kA adds nothing (its push is zero when the speed is not changing).

(b) During the ramp-down. Now the plan is lowering the speed, so the speed is changing. kA supplies the push for that change, but pointed to slow the robot down. kV keeps supplying the push for whatever speed the plan calls for at each instant, which is shrinking toward zero.

Pattern. kV tracks the plan's speed; kA tracks the plan's change in speed. The profile hands both of them a number for every instant, which is why feedforward can drive the whole trip without waiting for an error.

Example 3. A teammate says, "If Pedro Pathing plans the whole trip ahead of time, why do we still need the PID and the odometry? Just run the plan open loop." Sparky then hits a slightly slick tile and drifts a few inches off the path. What happens with plan-only, and what is the lesson?

What goes wrong plan-only. A plan run open loop just plays back the pushes it computed, with nothing watching the robot. When the slick tile makes Sparky drift, nothing notices and nothing corrects, so the robot finishes the plan a few inches off the scoring spot and the shot misses. This is the open-loop trap from Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop.

Why odometry and PID save it. Two-wheel odometry measures where the robot really is, PID compares that to where the plan says it should be, and it trims the small drift back onto the path. Feedforward from the plan does most of the driving; feedback handles the surprises the plan could not predict.

Aha: the plan is a prediction, and predictions are never perfect. Feedforward follows the plan; feedback catches what the plan missed. Path following needs both, which is why Pedro Pathing runs the PID and reads odometry the whole time.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking the profile replaces feedback. It does not. The plan plus feedforward gets you almost there, but a slick tile or a bump still needs PID and odometry to correct. Motion profiling is feedforward-led, not feedback-free.
  • Confusing the trapezoid axes. The trapezoid is speed vs. time, not position vs. time. Its height is speed and the area underneath is distance. Position over time is the smooth S-curve in the second figure.
  • Asking for a ramp the robot cannot do. If the plan's ramp-up is steeper than the motors can actually push, the robot falls behind its own plan and the error grows. The profile's cruise speed and ramp steepness have to fit what Sparky's drivetrain can really deliver.
  • Forgetting odometry is the eyes. Path following only works because two-wheel odometry tells the robot where it is on the field. Do not confuse it with motor encoders (those read motor speed and mechanism position, not field position).

17. Cascade Control: Nested Loops

Sometimes one control loop is not enough. You put a small, fast loop inside a bigger, slower one. The outer loop decides what you want; the inner loop makes it happen. Each loop has one clear job, so both are easier to tune.

Prerequisites: Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller
This chapter: Cascade Control: Nested Loops
Unlocks: Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism

One loop inside another

So far every controller you have met has been a single loop. It measures one thing, compares it to a target, and pushes. That works great for a flywheel or a simple turn. But some jobs are easier to split into two jobs.

Cascade control means putting one control loop inside another. "Cascade" just means one thing flows into the next, like water down a set of steps. The trick is simple to state:

The core idea: the OUTER loop's output becomes the INNER loop's setpoint. The outer loop does not drive the motor directly. Instead it hands a target to the inner loop, and the inner loop drives the motor.

Read that twice, because it is the whole chapter. The outer loop is the boss. It does not touch the motor. It just tells the inner loop what to aim for. The inner loop is the worker. It takes that aim and makes the motor do it.

The classic example: an arm moving to a position

Sparky has an arm that must swing to a set angle and stop there. (Next season it might be a linear slide instead. Same idea, so the lesson transfers.) You could try to control this with one big loop that looks at position and drives the motor. That can work, but it is fiddly to tune, because the same loop has to be gentle near the target and strong far away.

Cascade splits the job in two:

  • The OUTER loop watches POSITION. It asks "how far is the arm from where I want it?" When the arm is far away, it commands a high speed. When the arm is close, it commands a low speed. Its output is not a motor command. Its output is a speed to aim for.
  • The INNER loop watches SPEED. It takes that commanded speed as its own target and asks "is the motor actually spinning that fast?" It pushes the motor until the real speed matches the commanded speed.

So the outer loop says "move fast now, slow down as you get close," and the inner loop is the one that actually makes the motor spin at whatever speed it is told. The motor encoder does double duty here: it measures the motor speed for the inner loop and the arm position for the outer loop.

Far means fast, near means slow

The outer loop's job is easy to picture. It looks at the distance left to travel and picks a speed to command. Far from the target, command a high speed so the arm hurries over there. As the arm closes in, command a lower and lower speed, so it eases in gently and does not slam past. Right at the target, command zero speed, so it holds still.

This is really just proportional control (from Chapter 8) living in the outer loop: the commanded speed is proportional to how far off you are. The picture below shows the deal.

The rule: the inner loop must be faster

Here is the one rule you must remember about cascade control:

The rule: the INNER loop must be FASTER than the OUTER loop. The inner loop has to settle on its commanded speed quickly, before the outer loop changes its mind about what speed it wants.

Why does this matter? The outer loop is trusting the inner loop. Every time the outer loop says "spin at this speed," it assumes the inner loop will make that happen almost right away. If the inner loop is slow and sloppy, then by the time it finally reaches the commanded speed, the outer loop has already asked for a different one. The two loops end up fighting each other, and the whole thing wobbles.

Think of it like a manager and a worker. The manager (outer loop) keeps handing out new instructions. If the worker (inner loop) finishes each instruction quickly, the manager can trust them and keep steering. If the worker is slow, the instructions pile up and nothing gets done cleanly. So the inner loop has to be the quick one.

Everyday version: a driver and cruise control

You already understand cascade control, because cars use it. Picture driving on the highway.

  • You, the driver, are the outer loop. You look at the road far ahead and decide "I want to be going 65." You do not press the gas pedal by an exact amount yourself. You just pick a target speed.
  • Cruise control is the inner loop. You hand it that target speed. It watches the actual speed and works the throttle to hold 65, even up hills and down valleys.

Your job (pick the speed) and cruise control's job (hold the speed) are separate, and that is exactly why it works well. You think about the big picture; cruise control handles the details. And notice the speed rule: cruise control reacts to hills every second, while you only change your target speed once in a while. The inner loop is faster than the outer loop.

Why bother splitting it up?

You might ask: if one big loop can move the arm, why go to the trouble of two? Two reasons, and they are the whole point.

  • Each loop has one clear job. The outer loop only worries about position. The inner loop only worries about speed. A loop with one job is far easier to understand and to tune than a loop trying to do everything at once.
  • The inner loop hides the messy stuff. Motors are not perfect. Friction changes, the battery sags, an artifact adds weight. The inner speed loop absorbs all of that so that when the outer loop asks for a speed, it reliably gets that speed. The outer loop can then be simple, because it is standing on a solid inner loop it can trust.

In short, cascade control behaves better than one big loop and it is easier to tune, because you tune the fast inner loop first, get it solid, and then tune the slower outer loop on top of it.

Worked examples

Example 1. Theo is setting up cascade control on Sparky's arm. He has an outer position loop and an inner speed loop. His teammate says "let us tune the outer position loop first, then worry about the inner speed loop later." Why is that backwards?

The reason. The outer loop trusts the inner loop. When the outer loop commands a speed, it assumes the inner loop will deliver that speed almost right away. If the inner speed loop is not solid yet, then the outer loop is building on top of something wobbly, and no amount of outer-loop tuning will fix that.

The right order. Tune the inner speed loop first. Get it so that when you ask for a speed, the motor snaps to that speed quickly and holds it. Once the inner loop is fast and reliable, tune the outer position loop on top of it. Now the outer loop can trust every speed it commands.

Aha: always tune from the inside out. A cascade is only as good as its inner loop, so make the inner loop solid before you touch the outer one.

Example 2. Coach Brian asks: "In Sparky's arm cascade, the outer loop commands a speed of zero when the arm reaches the target. But the arm is heavy and gravity keeps pulling it down. What is the inner loop actually doing when the commanded speed is zero?"

Think about the two jobs. A commanded speed of zero means "do not move." It does not mean "turn the motor off." The inner speed loop's job is to make the real speed match the commanded speed, and here the commanded speed is zero.

So the inner loop holds still. If gravity starts to drag the arm down, the motor encoder sees a small downward speed. That is no longer zero, so the inner loop pushes back just enough to bring the speed back to zero and keep the arm from sliding. The arm holds its position because the inner loop is actively holding its speed at zero.

Connection. On a real vertical arm you would usually also add a gravity feedforward (the kG term from Chapter 13) so the motor holds against gravity without waiting for the arm to droop first. Cascade and feedforward work together.

Example 3. A teammate builds one giant single loop for the arm and it keeps overshooting the target and wobbling. He asks whether switching to cascade control would help, and why.

Why cascade can help. In a single big loop, the same set of numbers has to be strong enough to move the arm fast when it is far away and gentle enough not to slam past when it is close. That is hard to balance, and it often ends in overshoot. Cascade splits that tension. The outer loop naturally commands a high speed when far and a low speed when near, so the arm slows down on its own as it approaches.

The other win. Because the inner speed loop absorbs friction and battery sag, the outer loop gets predictable behavior to build on. Two simple, well-behaved loops usually beat one complicated loop that is trying to do everything. Just remember the rule: the inner loop must be the faster of the two.

Common pitfalls:
  • Making the outer loop faster than the inner loop. This breaks cascade control. If the outer loop changes its command faster than the inner loop can keep up, the two fight and the system wobbles. The inner loop must always be the quick one.
  • Thinking the outer loop drives the motor. It does not. The outer loop's only output is a target (here, a commanded speed) that it hands to the inner loop. Only the inner loop touches the motor.
  • Tuning the outer loop first. Always tune from the inside out. A shaky inner loop makes the outer loop impossible to tune well.
  • Using cascade everywhere. Cascade is worth it when a job splits cleanly into two levels, like position over speed. A plain flywheel only needs one speed loop, so do not wrap it in an outer loop it does not need. More loops is not automatically better.

You now know every controller in the family. This is the cheat sheet for real robots: for each part of Sparky, which controller do you reach for, and why. The trick is simple. Match the controller to the JOB and to the LOAD.

Prerequisites: Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller · Chapter 15: Take-Back-Half · Chapter 16: Motion Profiling · Chapter 17: Cascade Control
This chapter: Recipe Per Mechanism: Combining Controllers

One robot, many jobs

Sparky is not one machine with one controller. It is a bunch of little machines bolted together, and each one has a different job. The flywheel holds a speed. The drivetrain drives to a spot. The intake just turns on and off. Coach Brian likes to say each part is a different problem, so each part gets a different tool.

Theo already met all the tools. Bang-bang and hysteresis (Chapter 6, Chapter 7), PID (Chapter 12), feedforward and PIDF (Chapter 13), Take-Back-Half (Chapter 15), motion profiling (Chapter 16), and cascade loops (Chapter 17). This chapter does not teach a new controller. It teaches how to pick the right one, and how to stack a few together, for each real part of the robot.

The whole idea: to pick a controller, ask two questions. First, what is the JOB? On or off? Hold a speed? Hit a position? Follow a path? Aim? Second, what is the LOAD? Is something like gravity always fighting you, or not? The JOB tells you which controller. The LOAD tells you which feedforward term to add.

Walk through the rows

Drivetrain: two very different jobs

The mecanum drivetrain does two jobs, and they need different tools. Do not use one controller for both.

Drive to a spot. When Sparky must get from the start line to a shooting spot, that is a position job. The sensor is two-wheel odometry, which tells the robot its X and Y on the field. You do not just slam full power and hope. You plan a smooth path (speed up, cruise, slow down), let feedforward drive along the plan, and let PID fix the small error. In FTC the Pedro Pathing library does all of this for you. That is motion profiling plus feedforward plus PID, packaged. See Chapter 16.

Drive straight or hold a heading. This is a different job. Here you only care about which way the robot faces, measured by the IMU in degrees. A PD controller is perfect. P turns the robot toward the target heading, and D keeps it from swinging past and wobbling. There is no constant load pulling the heading off, so you usually do not need an I term. See Chapter 10.

Flywheel: speed wants feedforward

The flywheel holds a target RPM so every shot flies the same distance. The sensor is the motor encoder. A speed job loves feedforward, because holding a speed needs a steady push, and you already know roughly how big that push is. The kV feedforward term predicts it and applies it up front, so the wheel snaps to speed and recovers fast when an artifact drags it down. That is PIDF. A simpler team can use Take-Back-Half, which holds a speed well with almost no tuning, or plain bang-bang if they just need it close. Chapter 19 races all three head to head.

Why velocity plus feedforward wins here. A flywheel never sits still. It must keep spinning, which means it always needs a push, even when the error is zero. Plain PID goes quiet at zero error, so it keeps falling behind and catching up. Feedforward (kV) keeps supplying the holding push based on the target RPM, so the wheel stays at speed and PID only patches the dips. That is why speed jobs are feedforward-first.

Aim: no turret, rotate the whole robot

Sparky's shooter is fixed. It does not swivel. There is no turret. So how do you aim? You spin the whole robot. The mecanum drivetrain can rotate in place, so the robot turns until it points at the goal.

The clever part is the camera. The Limelight sits directly under the shooter and faces the same way. So when the camera is centered on the goal, the shooter is aimed at the goal too. The camera measures how far off-center the goal is, in degrees. That offset is exactly the aim error. Feed that error into a P or PD controller that rotates the robot, and it spins until the error is zero, which means the shooter is on target. See Chapter 20.

Do not overthink aiming. It is just a heading job in disguise. The camera hands you the error already measured. You are back to the same PD-on-a-heading idea as driving straight, only now the target comes from the camera instead of a fixed number. No turret, no extra motor, just rotate the robot until the camera is centered.

Intake and gate: just on and off

The intake grabs artifacts, and the classifier gate holds and releases them. These are simple two-state actors. They are on, or they are off. A full PID would be silly here. Use bang-bang: turn it on until the job is done, then off. If it flickers on and off too fast at the edge (chatter), add a small deadband, which is hysteresis (Chapter 7). Match the controller to the job, and a two-state job gets a two-state controller.

Position systems and linear slides: the load decides the feedforward

Now the part that trips up a lot of teams, and the reason this chapter keeps saying "check the LOAD." DECODE does not use linear slides, but almost every other season does, so this transfers. A linear slide is a lift that extends a scoring part in and out. The job is a position, measured in inches by the motor encoder. Both a vertical and a horizontal slide are position jobs, so both use PID at the core. But the feedforward term is different, and the difference is gravity.

A vertical slide lifts straight up. Gravity pulls it down the whole time, right along the direction it moves. So gravity is fighting the job every second, even when the slide is parked. You must add a kG feedforward term to hold the weight up. Without kG the slide droops below its target, exactly the steady-state sag you saw in Chapter 13. Teams often add a little kS too, to break the starting friction.

A horizontal slide extends sideways. Gravity still pulls down, but now that pull is across the direction of motion, not against it. The rail carries the weight, so gravity does not drag the slide back toward the start. There is nothing to hold up along the travel, so you add no kG. You still add kS to break the stickiness and get it moving. Same PID core, but the feedforward changes because the load changed.

Aha: the PID does not change between the two slides. Only the feedforward changes. Ask "does gravity fight the direction this thing moves?" If yes, add kG. If no, skip kG. This one question saves you from a drooping lift or a wasted term.
Want even tighter position control? Use a cascade. For a precise slide or arm, some teams nest two loops (Chapter 17). An outer position loop looks at how far the slide is from its target height and asks for a speed. An inner velocity loop makes the motor hit that speed. The outer loop aims, the inner loop drives, and the inner one must be faster. This is optional, but it is how you get very smooth, very accurate positioning.

The decision picture: job first, then load

Here is the whole choosing process as one flow. Start at the top and ask what the JOB is. That points you to a controller. If the job is hitting a position, ask one more question about the LOAD to pick the feedforward term.

Worked examples

Example 1. Coach Brian says, "Next season the game needs a vertical lift to hang scoring pieces up high. What controller stack should we start with, and which single term matters most?" Theo has to answer.

Ask the job. A lift moves to and holds a height, measured in inches by the motor encoder. That is a position job, so the core is PID.

Ask the load. The lift is vertical, so gravity pulls straight down, right against the up motion. Gravity fights the job the whole time, even when the lift is parked. So add the kG gravity feedforward term, and usually a little kS to break starting friction.

The stack. PID + kG (+ kS). The term that matters most is kG. Without it, the lift droops below its target and PID has to fight gravity forever with a leftover error. With kG holding the weight, PID only trims. This is the vertical slide row of the recipe table.

Aha: position job means PID. Gravity fighting the motion means add kG. Two questions, one answer.

Example 2. In a DECODE match, Sparky drives to its shooting spot, then needs to aim at the goal before it fires. A rookie asks, "Do we need a turret motor to point the shooter?" What is the right answer, and what controller aims the robot?

No turret. Sparky's shooter is fixed and does not swivel. To aim, the robot rotates its whole body. The mecanum drivetrain can spin in place, so it turns until it points at the goal.

Where the error comes from. The Limelight camera sits under the shooter and faces the same way, so when the camera is centered on the goal, the shooter is aimed. The camera reports the aim offset in degrees. That offset is the error.

The controller. Feed that error into a P or PD controller that rotates the robot. P spins toward the goal, D stops it from wobbling past. When the error hits zero, the camera is centered and the shot is lined up. It is the same idea as holding a heading with the IMU, only the target comes from the camera.

Example 3. The next season's robot uses the SAME lift hardware, but now it is mounted flat, extending horizontally across the field to grab a piece. Theo already tuned the vertical version with PID + kG + kS. What should he change for the horizontal version, and why?

Keep the PID. It is still a position job, moving to a spot in inches. The PID core does not change.

Drop the kG. Now the slide moves sideways, and gravity pulls down, across the direction of travel, not against it. The rail carries the weight, so gravity never drags the slide back toward the start. There is nothing to hold up along the motion, so kG is not needed and should be removed. Leaving it in would make the slide creep in one direction for no reason.

Keep the kS. The slide still has starting friction to break, so keep the small kS push to get it moving smoothly.

Aha: the controller followed the load, not the hardware. Same lift, but rotating it from vertical to horizontal removed the gravity fight, so the kG term goes away. Always ask "does gravity fight this motion?" before you pick the feedforward.
Common pitfalls:
  • Using one controller for the whole robot. Each mechanism is a different job. A flywheel is not a drivetrain is not an intake. Match the controller to each part.
  • Reaching for full PID on an on/off job. An intake or gate has two states. Bang-bang (with hysteresis if it chatters) is the right, simple tool. PID is wasted effort here.
  • Forgetting kG on a vertical slide. The slide will droop below its target and never quite hold. Gravity fighting the motion means you need kG.
  • Adding kG to a horizontal slide. Gravity does not fight sideways motion, so a kG term just shoves the slide in one direction with no reason. Use kS instead, no kG.
  • Thinking aiming needs a turret. Sparky has no turret. Aiming is a heading job solved by rotating the whole robot, using the camera offset as the error.
  • Slamming full power to drive to a spot. A big move needs a planned, smooth path (motion profiling via Pedro Pathing), not a raw jump that overshoots.

19. Payoff: The Flywheel Shooter

Hold the shooter at a target RPM so every artifact flies the same distance. This is the real problem the whole course was building toward, and now we solve it three ways and watch them race on one graph.

Prerequisites: Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism
Builds on: Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control · Chapter 15: Take-Back-Half · Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF
Unlocks: Chapter 20: Payoff, Aiming by Rotating the Robot

This is the chapter the course has been pointing at since Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control. You have met every controller in the family: bang-bang, P, PD, PI, PID, feedforward, and Take-Back-Half. Now you take ONE real DECODE problem and solve it three different ways. You will see all three side by side, on the same graph, and you will finally see why one of them wins. No new theory here. This is where the theory pays off.

The real problem: hold 3000 RPM

Sparky, your team's robot, shoots 5-inch artifacts into the goal with a spinning wheel called a flywheel. The wheel flings the artifact. How fast the artifact leaves depends on how fast the wheel is spinning. So here is the rule that decides whether you score: for every shot to fly the same distance, the wheel must spin at the same speed every time.

Say your team has picked a target of 3000 RPM. RPM means rotations per minute, a measure of how fast the wheel spins. If the wheel is at 3000 RPM when the artifact leaves, the artifact lands in the goal. If the wheel is a little slow, the artifact lands short. If the wheel is a little fast, it flies long. So the job of the controller is simple to say and hard to do: keep the flywheel at 3000 RPM, no matter what.

Two things make this hard, and both are old friends from earlier chapters:

  • The wheel is never perfectly steady on its own. Battery voltage sags, the motor warms up, friction changes. So an open-loop "just send 60 percent power" (Chapter 3: Open Loop vs Closed Loop) will drift off 3000 RPM. You need a closed loop: measure, compare, correct (Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop).
  • Feeding an artifact is a disturbance. The moment a 5-inch artifact touches the spinning wheel, it steals energy from the wheel and the speed drops. In a match you shoot artifact after artifact, so the wheel gets hit again and again. The controller has to recover from each hit fast, before the next shot.
Aha: the flywheel is a speed-holding job, not a "get there once" job. It is not enough to reach 3000 RPM. You have to stay at 3000 while artifacts keep knocking you down. Staying steady under repeated hits is the whole game.

Three controllers, one problem

You already know three tools that can do this job. They are not equally good, but they all work, and seeing them race is the best way to feel why we bothered learning the fancy ones. Here they are in order of increasing cleverness.

Solution 1: Bang-bang (the blunt one)

Start with the controller you met first, in Chapter 6: Bang-Bang Control. The rule is as blunt as it gets. Look at the sign of the error, and pick one of two outputs:

  • Wheel spinning below 3000 RPM → motor full power.
  • Wheel spinning above 3000 RPM → motor off.

Remember the definition of error from every controller chapter: error is the target minus the measurement.

\[ \text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measurement} = 3000 - (\text{real RPM}) \]

Bang-bang looks only at whether that error is positive or negative. It ignores how big it is. This really works, and some FTC teams really do run their flywheel this way. It is almost impossible to break, and there is nothing to tune.

But it ripples. This is the honest catch you learned in Chapter 6, and it matters here. The instant the wheel reaches 3000, the controller cuts power, so the wheel starts slowing. The instant it dips below 3000, full power slams back on, so it climbs past 3000 again. There is no output that says "hold right here." So the speed is always either climbing or falling. It draws a small saw-tooth, wandering a bit above and a bit below 3000, forever. A wheel bouncing between 2950 and 3050 RPM shoots artifacts that fly slightly different distances.

And after an artifact hits, it keeps sawing. When the driver feeds an artifact, the wheel drops hard, say to 2650 RPM. Bang-bang does the one thing it can do: full power. That is actually a strong recovery, because full power is the most push it could ever apply. The wheel climbs back fast. But once it gets back near 3000, it does not settle. It just returns to the same saw-tooth it had before. So bang-bang recovers, but it never gives you a flat, steady speed.

Solution 2: Take-Back-Half (the simple settler)

Now bring in Take-Back-Half, or TBH, from Chapter 15: Take-Back-Half. TBH was invented for exactly this job: holding a flywheel at a steady speed. It is almost as simple to set up as bang-bang, with essentially one tuning number and no derivative to fuss over. But unlike bang-bang, it can actually settle on a flat speed instead of sawing forever.

Here is the idea in plain words. While the wheel is below 3000, TBH slowly builds up the motor power, adding up the error as it goes. The clever move happens every time the speed crosses the target. The instant the wheel passes 3000 on the way up, TBH "takes back half": it drops the power halfway back toward the value it had at the last crossing. Halving the swing at every crossing kills the wobble. Instead of over- and under-shooting forever like bang-bang, the wheel spirals inward and parks on the one steady power that holds 3000.

Aha: TBH is the sweet spot between simple and good. Almost no tuning, like bang-bang, but a flat steady speed, like the fancier controllers. When an artifact hits, TBH feels the error, powers back up, and settles onto 3000 again. Its dip is a bit deeper and its recovery a bit slower than the best controller, because TBH has no way to predict the power it needs. But it is a huge step up from an endless saw-tooth.

Solution 3: PIDF (the predict-then-trim one)

Now use the controller from Chapter 13: Feedforward and PIDF. The key idea is that a flywheel is a speed job, and for a speed job we can predict the power we need before any error shows up.

The prediction is the feedforward term. For velocity, the useful one is kV: the output that is proportional to the target speed. It answers "roughly how much motor power does it take to spin this wheel at 3000 RPM?" You measure that once during tuning (Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller) and bake it in. Then, from the very first instant, the controller sends about the right power straight from the target. It does not wait for an error. That is what makes feedforward different from feedback.

The PID part is the trim. Feedforward gets you most of the way, but not perfectly, because the real robot is a little different from the number you measured. So the PID (Chapter 12: PID Together) watches the leftover error and nudges the power up or down to erase it. Put together, PIDF is:

The PIDF split: feedforward predicts the power for 3000 RPM (most of the work). PID reacts to whatever error is left and trims it away (the cleanup). Predict most, react to the rest.

The money shot: watch all three side by side

Here is the whole point of the chapter in one picture. Same flywheel, same 3000 RPM target, same artifact fed at the same moment. The only difference is the controller.

Read the graph slowly, because it says everything:

  • Before the artifact: bang-bang saws between about 2950 and 3050 RPM. TBH and PIDF both sit flat on 3000. This is the first big split: a controller that can settle beats one that only saws.
  • When the artifact hits: bang-bang drops far, to about 2650. TBH dips to about 2820. PIDF dips the least, to about 2900. PIDF barely flinches, partly because the feedforward is still holding most of the power the whole time.
  • After the artifact: bang-bang recovers hard (full power is a strong recovery) but then returns to its saw-tooth. TBH climbs back and settles calmly onto 3000. PIDF snaps back to flat fastest of all and stays there.

Why PIDF wins here (the real reason)

It is tempting to say "PIDF wins because it is fancier." That is not the reason, and if you say that in your talk someone will ask "fancier how?" The real reason is specific to this job:

The reason: speed control has a known feedforward. You can predict, ahead of time, roughly how much power it takes to spin the wheel at 3000 RPM (that is what kV is). So PIDF spends most of its effort predicting the right power and only a little reacting to error. TBH has no prediction, so it has to feel the error and build the power back up each time. Bang-bang can only react, and only in the crudest way (full on or full off). Predicting beats reacting when the prediction is good, and for a flywheel the prediction is very good.

Put another way: bang-bang finds 3000 RPM by constantly overshooting and undershooting it, because it has no idea what power 3000 needs. It hunts for it. TBH is smarter, it settles, but it still has to discover the right power from scratch each time it gets knocked off. PIDF already knows the answer, applies it, and only fixes the small leftover. That is why the PIDF line dips least and recovers first.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's flywheel target is 3000 RPM. It is running steadily between shots. The motor encoder reads 2970 RPM. What does bang-bang do, and what does PIDF do, at this instant?

Bang-bang. The error is \(3000 - 2970 = +30\) RPM, which is positive, so the wheel is below target. Bang-bang looks only at the sign. Positive means full power. Notice it slams to full power even though you are only 30 RPM low. That overreaction is exactly why it will shoot past 3000 and keep sawing.

PIDF. The feedforward is already sending most of the power for 3000 RPM, and it does not change based on this reading at all, because feedforward does not use error. The PID part sees the small \(+30\) error and adds a small trim, sized to that small error (that is the P term from Chapter 8: Proportional Control). So the total power ticks up just a little, and the wheel eases back to 3000 without blowing past it.

Aha: for the same 30 RPM miss, bang-bang applies its maximum, and PIDF applies a gentle nudge on top of a good prediction. The gentle nudge is why PIDF settles flat and bang-bang cannot.

Example 2. Mid-match, the driver feeds an artifact into Sparky's shooter. The artifact drags the wheel from a steady 3000 RPM down to 2650 RPM in an instant. Walk through how each of the three controllers recovers.

The disturbance. The artifact is a disturbance (Chapter 2: Robots Are Messy). It knocks the speed down to 2650, so the error jumps to \(3000 - 2650 = +350\) RPM, strongly positive.

Bang-bang. It slams to full power, because it is below target. This is a strong, fast recovery, because full power is the most it can ever apply. The wheel climbs back toward 3000. But the moment it arrives, bang-bang has nothing that says "hold here." It cuts power, dips, powers up, and returns to its normal saw-tooth. It recovers, but it never gives you flat.

Take-Back-Half. It feels the big error and builds power back up, and as the wheel crosses 3000 again it takes back half, so the overshoot shrinks and it settles calmly back onto 3000. It dips more than PIDF and takes a little longer, because it has no prediction holding the wheel up during the hit. But it ends flat, not sawing.

PIDF. Two things help at once. First, the feedforward never stopped sending the power for 3000 RPM, so the wheel was already being held up the whole time. The artifact only knocked it off the prediction, it did not remove the prediction. Second, the PID sees the big \(+350\) error and adds a big trim on top, pushing the wheel back up fast. As the error shrinks, the trim shrinks too, so the wheel eases back onto 3000 without a big overshoot, and holds flat. That is the small dip and quick recovery you see in the graph.

Aha: PIDF recovers best not because its reaction is stronger, but because it never let go of the prediction. Feedforward is holding the floor up while PID climbs back onto it. TBH and bang-bang both have to rebuild the power from nothing.

Example 3. Coach Brian asks Theo: "Bang-bang, TBH, and PIDF all recover from a big hit eventually. So why does bang-bang shoot the least consistently across a whole match?" Answer him.

The recovery from a single big hit is not the deciding factor. The difference is what happens in between shots, which is most of the match. Bang-bang can never sit still. Even with no artifact touching it, it saws between about 2950 and 3050 RPM, so back-to-back shots fired at slightly different points in the saw-tooth fly slightly different distances. TBH and PIDF both hold a flat line between shots, so every shot leaves at almost exactly 3000 RPM. Consistency across many shots is decided by the steady state, not by the one dramatic recovery. Bang-bang loses the steady state, and that is why it scatters.

Aha: a match is mostly the quiet moments between shots. The controllers that are calmest when nothing special is happening (TBH and PIDF) are the ones that score consistently.

Why consistent RPM is the whole point

Everything above is about holding a flat speed. Here is why a team cares, in one picture: the speed of the wheel decides where the artifact lands.

The practical takeaway: which one, and when

All three work. Here is how to choose, and it is the same "pick the controller that fits the job" thinking from Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism:

  • PIDF is the default for the flywheel. It is the standard choice for FTC velocity control, and this chapter is why. Speed has a known feedforward (kV), so you get the flattest RPM and the fastest recovery after each artifact. Most FTC motor libraries have a built-in velocity PIDF mode, so you often just set the kV, kS, P, I, and D values rather than writing the loop by hand.
  • Take-Back-Half is the great middle option. If you want a flat, steady speed with almost no tuning, TBH is hard to beat. One constant, no derivative, and it settles. Reach for it when you do not have a clean way to measure kV, or when you just want something simple that works well. It gives up a little dip depth and recovery speed to PIDF, and that is a fair trade for the simplicity.
  • Bang-bang is the simplest backup, and the right tool elsewhere. If your library makes both PIDF and TBH hard, a bang-bang flywheel still works and still scores. And for true on/off jobs, like the intake or the classifier gate, bang-bang is the right tool, not a compromise.
  • Judge it by the graph, not by feel. Log the motor encoder RPM and plot it over a match, like the money figure above. A flat line through the shots means consistent scoring. A saw-tooth means you have controller or tuning work left to do.
Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking feedforward reacts to error. It does not. Feedforward predicts the power from the target and applies it no matter what the error is. The PID part does the reacting. If you mix these up, you will not understand why PIDF holds flat.
  • Skipping the feedforward and using pure PID on the flywheel. It can work, but you throw away the biggest advantage. Without kV, the PID has to build up the whole power from error alone, which is slower and dips more after each artifact. Predict first, then trim.
  • Expecting bang-bang to ever sit flat. It cannot, with or without an artifact. With only full-on and off, the speed is always climbing or falling. If you need a flat line, use TBH or PIDF.
  • Cranking gains to kill the dip after an artifact. A huge P or I makes the recovery snappier but brings back overshoot and oscillation (Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation). More gain is not always better. Fix the dip mostly with good feedforward, not by maxing the feedback.

P2. Theo switches Sparky's flywheel to PIDF for a 3000 RPM target. He measures the feedforward so that, on prediction alone, the wheel spins steadily at about 2950 RPM (close, but 50 RPM short). What is the PID part's job now, and which one term of P, I, or D is best at removing a small, steady 50 RPM gap like this? Explain why.

Answer

The PID's job is to trim the leftover error the prediction missed. Feedforward got the wheel to 2950. The error is \(3000 - 2950 = +50\) RPM, small and steady.

The best term is I (integral). A small, unchanging leftover gap is a steady-state error (Chapter 11: Integral Control). The P term alone would push a little for a 50 RPM error, but that small push often is not quite enough to fully close a steady gap, so a little error lingers. The I term adds up the leftover error over time and keeps pushing harder until the gap reaches zero. That is exactly what integral control is for: erasing a small steady miss. In practice you would use mostly feedforward, a little P, and a small I to clean up the last 50 RPM.

Pattern: a leftover gap that will not close on its own is the signature of steady-state error, and integral is the term that removes it. Reach for I when a steady offset remains after P has settled, and keep it small so it does not wind up and overshoot.

20. Payoff: Aiming by Rotating the Robot

The shooter is bolted on. So aiming means spinning the whole robot until the camera sees the goal dead center, then holding still to shoot.

Prerequisites: Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism (builds on Chapter 5: Sensors and Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD))
This chapter: Payoff: Aiming by Rotating the Robot
Unlocks: Chapter 21: Autonomous Driving, and Wrap-Up

This is a payoff chapter. There is nothing new to learn here. You already have every piece: the feedback loop, the camera, the error, and PD. This chapter snaps them together to solve one real DECODE job, aiming Sparky at the goal.

The real problem

The driver drives Sparky to a good spot to shoot. But the goal is almost never perfectly in front of the robot. Sparky rolls to its spot and the goal ends up a little off to one side. If the robot shoots while it is pointed even a few degrees off, the artifact sails wide and misses.

Here is the key fact about Sparky. The shooter is fixed. It is bolted straight to the frame and cannot swivel on its own. So how do you aim a shooter that cannot turn?

You turn the whole robot. Sparky has a mecanum drivetrain, so it can spin in place, like a chair spinning on one spot. Theo aims by rotating the entire robot until it points straight at the goal, then holding it there so every shot flies true.

And there is a second key fact. The Limelight camera is mounted directly under the shooter, facing the same way. The camera and the shooter look in the exact same direction. That is the whole trick of this chapter, so hold onto it: when the camera is centered on the goal, the shooter is aimed at the goal. Center the camera and you have aimed the gun for free.

Aha: the camera and the shooter are co-aligned, meaning they point the same way. So "aim the shooter" becomes "get the goal centered in the camera." You never aim the shooter directly. You just spin the robot until the camera says the goal is dead center.

The camera hands you the error for free

Recall from Chapter 5 what a Limelight-style camera reports when it looks at the goal. It does not give a distance in inches or a compass heading. It reports one number: how far off-center the goal is in the picture.

If the goal is dead center, that number is zero. If the goal is off to the right, the number is positive. Off to the left, it is negative. The number can be in degrees or in pixels. Either way it means "how far sideways the goal sits from straight ahead."

Here is the payoff. The whole point of aiming is to get the goal centered. Centered means the off-center amount is zero. So the target for this job is simply offset = 0. Remember the error from Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop: error is the target minus the measurement.

\[ \text{error} = \text{target} - \text{measurement} = 0 - \text{offset} \]

Because the target is zero, the error is just the offset itself, with a sign that tells the robot which way to spin. You do not have to compute an error at all. The camera's off-center number already is the error. The robot only has to spin until that number reaches zero.

Aha: most control problems make you build the error by subtracting a measurement from a target. Aiming is easier: the camera measures the offset, the target offset is zero, so the reading you get back is the error. Drive it to zero and the co-aligned shooter is on the goal.

This is a position job, so it is a job for P and PD

Spinning to face the goal is a position job. The thing you are controlling is an angle: which way the robot is pointing. That is exactly the kind of job you learned P and PD on, back in Chapter 8: Proportional Control (P) and Chapter 10: Derivative Control (PD), when you spun the robot to a target heading with the IMU.

Aiming with the camera is that same job with one change. Instead of the gyro telling you the heading, the camera tells you the offset. The controller does not care where the number comes from. It just shrinks the error to zero.

So the natural first try is proportional control. Big offset, big spin. Small offset, gentle spin. Zero offset, stop. That eases the robot toward the goal instead of slamming toward it. And from your recipe work in Chapter 18, you already tag a "point at an angle" job as a P-or-PD problem. Aiming is one more of those.

Why P alone is not enough: the whole robot has momentum

Here is where the trouble starts, and it is the exact trouble from Chapter 9: Overshoot and Oscillation. A spinning robot has momentum. Momentum means a moving thing wants to keep moving. It cannot stop instantly. And remember, you are not spinning a light little part here. You are spinning the entire robot, which is heavy. That is a lot of momentum to stop.

So even when proportional control eases the push near center, the robot's own spin carries it a little past. The goal swings from the right side of the frame, through center, and out to the left side. Then P sees a new error the other way and spins back, and the goal swings past center again. The robot hunts back and forth across the goal.

That hunting is bad for a very concrete reason: you might shoot while the robot is still spinning. If Sparky fires during the swing, the artifact leaves while the whole robot is sweeping sideways, so it flies off at an angle and misses. You need the robot to settle and hold still on the goal before it shoots.

You already know the fix. It is the derivative term from Chapter 10, the anticipating brake. The derivative term watches how fast the offset is shrinking. As the goal races toward center, the offset is closing fast, so the derivative term eases off early, like braking before a stop sign. The robot slows into center and settles instead of blowing through it. Proportional plus derivative is PD, and PD is what makes a camera aimer settle fast and hold still.

The aiming loop, start to finish

Put it in the feedback loop shape from Chapter 4, with the camera as the "measure" box and the whole robot as the thing being moved. Nothing here is new. It is the same loop you have drawn all course, wired to the camera and to the drivetrain.

Trace one trip around. The camera sees the goal 8 degrees to the right, so the error is 8 degrees off. The PD controller spins the robot toward the goal, hard at first because the error is big, then easing as the derivative brake feels the goal rushing toward center. The camera looks again and reports a smaller offset. Around and around, dozens of times a second, until the offset is zero and Sparky holds on the goal. Then the driver shoots.

Two practical realities to respect

Two honest facts about the camera keep this from being as clean as spinning to a gyro heading. Both come straight from earlier chapters. Aiming just makes them concrete.

  • The camera is a little noisy. As you saw in Chapter 5, no sensor reading is perfectly smooth. The off-center number jitters a few tenths of a degree even when the robot is holding still. And from Chapter 10, you know the derivative term reacts to how fast the reading changes, so it turns that jitter into a twitchy output. If you crank the derivative gain too high, the robot buzzes and jerks while it is supposed to be sitting quietly on the goal. So use enough D to stop the wobble, but do not crank it up.
  • The camera updates at a limited rate. A camera does not report a fresh number as fast as a motor encoder does. It sends a new reading only so many times a second, with a small delay while it processes each picture. That means the aiming loop gets fresh error a bit slower than a gyro loop would. A slower, delayed reading is one more reason not to crank the derivative gain high: there is less clean, up-to-date information for it to work with, so a huge D just amplifies the jumps between updates.

The takeaway is the same tuning lesson from Chapter 14: Tuning a Controller: enough P to spin briskly, enough D to stop the swing, and no more D than that. On a noisy, slower sensor like the camera, "no more than that" matters even more. And when the offset finally holds at zero, remember what that means: the fixed shooter, co-aligned with the camera, is on target.

Where aiming shows up in a DECODE match

This is not a drill. Sparky aims by rotating in the real game. During the 30-second autonomous period, the camera first reads the obelisk, which shows the match's random three-piece pattern using AprilTags (the printed markers from Chapter 5). That tells the robot which pattern to score.

Then the same camera switches to the goal, reports the off-center amount, and the PD aiming loop spins Sparky to face it before it shoots its matching artifacts. Read the pattern, spin to aim, shoot, all on the robot's own, with no driver. That autonomous story is where Chapter 21: Autonomous Driving, and Wrap-Up picks up.

Worked examples

Example 1. Sparky's camera reports the goal is 12 degrees off-center to the right. The target is for the goal to be centered. Theo says "the controller needs to compute the error." Coach Brian says "you already have it." Who is right, and what is the error?

Find the target and the measurement. The target is a centered goal, which means an off-center amount of 0. The measurement is what the camera reports, which is 12 degrees off-center.

Compute the error. Error is target minus measurement, so \(0 - 12 = -12\) degrees. The size is 12 degrees, and the sign says the goal is on one side, so spin that way to shrink it.

Who is right. Coach Brian. Because the target offset is zero, the error is just the camera's number, with a sign. There is no separate reading to subtract. The camera hands you the error directly, so the robot simply spins to make that number zero.

Aha: aiming is the one job where the sensor reading already is the error, because the target is zero. That is what makes the camera such a natural fit for a feedback loop.

Example 2. Theo aims Sparky with proportional control only. The robot spins toward the goal fast, but the goal shoots past center to the other side of the frame, comes back, overshoots again, and the robot keeps hunting back and forth for a full second before it holds. The driver fires during the hunt and the shot misses wide. Name what is happening, why it makes shots miss, and the fix.

What is happening. This is overshoot and oscillation from Chapter 9. The spinning robot has momentum, and it is the whole heavy robot spinning, not some light little part. So even as proportional control eases the push near center, the robot's spin carries it past. Then P corrects the other way and it overshoots again. The goal keeps swinging across center, which is the hunting.

Why shots miss. The robot is still spinning when the driver shoots. Because the shooter is bolted to the frame, a moving frame means a moving shooter. An artifact fired while the whole robot is sweeping sideways leaves at an angle and flies wide. You can only shoot accurately once the robot has settled and is holding still on the goal.

The fix. Add a derivative term to make it PD, from Chapter 10. The derivative brake senses the offset closing fast and eases off early, so the robot slows into center and settles instead of swinging past. PD holds still on the goal, which is what makes the shot safe.

Aha: the danger of hunting is not just that it looks bad. It is that you might shoot mid-swing, and with a fixed shooter a mid-swing shot always flies off at an angle. PD earns its place here by making the robot hold still before it fires.

Example 3. Sparky now aims with PD and holds on the goal, but the driver notices the robot buzzes and jerks a little while it sits on the goal waiting to shoot. Theo had turned the derivative gain up very high to kill the last of the swing, and this is a camera loop, not a gyro loop. What is going on, and what should he do?

What is going on. The robot is holding still, so the real offset is basically zero. But the camera reading is a little noisy (Chapter 5), and it jitters a few tenths of a degree even when nothing is moving. The camera also updates slower than a motor encoder, so the jumps between readings look sharp. The derivative term reacts to how fast the reading changes (Chapter 10), and a very high derivative gain multiplies those small, sharp jitters into big up-and-down drivetrain commands. That is the buzz. The controller is chasing camera noise, not real motion.

What to do. Turn the derivative gain back down. Use just enough D to stop the swing on a real aim, and no more. Because the camera is noisier and slower than a gyro, the safe ceiling for D is lower here than it was for spinning to a gyro heading. Teams also smooth the camera reading before the derivative term sees it.

Pattern, match the D to the sensor: the same PD idea can need a gentler derivative gain on a noisy, slower sensor than on a clean, fast one. When you move a controller from the gyro to the camera, re-tune. Do not assume the gains that worked on one sensor are safe on another.

Common pitfalls:
  • Thinking you aim the shooter. You do not. The shooter is fixed and cannot swivel. You aim by spinning the whole robot on its mecanum wheels until the co-aligned camera is centered on the goal.
  • Thinking aiming needs a special new controller. It does not. Aiming is a position job, so it is plain P and PD from Chapters 8 and 10, with the camera's offset as the error.
  • Forgetting that the whole robot has momentum. P alone overshoots because the heavy robot cannot stop spinning instantly. If you expect P to land perfectly, you will be surprised by the hunting. That is why you add D.
  • Shooting before the robot settles. A shot fired mid-spin flies wide, because the fixed shooter moves with the frame. Wait until the offset is at zero and holding before you fire.
  • Cranking D up because the camera is jumpy. A noisy, slower sensor needs less derivative gain, not more. Too much D turns camera noise into a buzzing, twitchy robot.

P3. Theo moves his aiming controller from the gyro (a fast, fairly clean sensor) to the camera (a slower, noisier sensor). He keeps the exact same PD gains that worked great on the gyro. Part A: The autonomous payoff

The 30 seconds with no driver

Every DECODE match begins with a 30-second autonomous period. During these 30 seconds the drivers cannot touch the robot. Sparky has to sense the field, decide what to do, and do it, all on its own. Here is what it must pull off in that half minute:

  • Read the obelisk. Before the match, the obelisk shows a random three-piece pattern (the motif) using AprilTags. Sparky's camera reads which pattern it is.
  • Leave the start line and drive to a shooting spot. It must reach an exact place on the field and face the right way.
  • Aim and shoot the matching artifacts. Artifacts are the 5-inch plastic game pieces. Sparky spins its flywheel up to speed, turns to line up on the goal, and shoots the ones that match the pattern.

Notice that this is not one job. It is several of the ideas from this whole course, running together. The exciting part is that you have already met every single piece. This chapter just wires them into one routine.

The closed loops Sparky runs

Watch what "drive out and shoot on your own" really is. It is not one giant program. It is a stack of the closed loops from this course, each one the same four-step idea from Chapter 4: The Feedback Loop: pick a target, measure, find the error, correct, repeat. They just watch different quantities with different sensors. Here are the loops, in the order Sparky uses them.

Loop 1: drive to a spot (two-wheel odometry + Pedro Pathing). The target is a place on the field, given in inches, say the shooting spot 48 inches out. The sensor is two-wheel odometry from Chapter 5: two unpowered tracking wheels plus the IMU that together report where the robot actually is on the field. Notice this is not the motor encoders. Motor encoders count how fast a motor spins; they do not know the robot's position on the field. Odometry is the sensor for "where am I." The controller here is Pedro Pathing from Chapter 16. It plans a smooth motion profile (ramp up speed, cruise, ramp down and stop right on the mark), uses feedforward to drive along that plan, and lets PID correct the small leftover error. That is a full PIDF-style path follower for the mecanum drivetrain.

Loop 2: hold a heading (the IMU). While it drives, Sparky must also face the right way. The target is a heading in degrees, say 0 degrees straight down the field. The sensor is the IMU (gyro) from Chapter 5, which measures which way the robot points. The error is the target heading minus the measured heading. This is a PD job: P nudges the robot back toward the target angle, and D keeps it from wobbling as it corrects. This runs at the same time as Loop 1, on the same mecanum drivetrain. Pedro Pathing actually folds position and heading together, but it helps to picture them as two loops sharing the wheels.

Loop 3: flywheel at speed (the motor encoder). Before Sparky can shoot, the flywheel must be spinning at its target speed so every shot flies the same distance. The target is an RPM, say 3000. The sensor is the motor encoder from Chapter 5, which counts the wheel's rotation to give its speed. The controller is PIDF from Chapter 13 (or Take-Back-Half from Chapter 15). This is the exact loop from the flywheel payoff in Chapter 19.

Loop 4: aim by rotating the whole robot (the camera). Sparky's shooter is fixed. There is no turret. The camera is mounted right under the shooter and faces the same way, so when the camera is centered on the goal, the shooter is aimed. To aim, Sparky rotates its whole body (the mecanum drive can spin in place) until the camera sees the goal centered. The target is zero off-center. The sensor is the camera plus AprilTag, which measures how far off-center the goal is. The controller is a P (or PD) loop that turns that off-center amount into a rotate command. This is the aiming payoff from Chapter 20.

Aha: autonomous is not one big controller. It is four simple loops you already understand, each with the sensor that measures its own quantity: odometry for where the robot is, the IMU for which way it faces, the motor encoder for flywheel speed, and the camera for how far off-center the goal is. One sensor, one quantity, one loop.

The full story: sense, decide, act

Now zoom back out to the whole 30 seconds. Every robot behavior in this course, and really in all of robotics, follows the same three beats. Autonomous is the clearest place to see all three at once.

  • Sense. The camera reads the obelisk's AprilTag and reports which of the three-piece patterns is showing. From Chapter 5, remember this is the camera's "read a label" job, not its "how far off-center" job. Sparky now knows the pattern.
  • Decide. Sparky picks the artifacts that match that pattern. This is plain logic, an "if the pattern is this, shoot those artifacts" choice. No controller here, just a decision based on what it sensed.
  • Act. Sparky drives to the shooting spot (odometry plus Pedro Pathing, holding heading with the IMU), aims by rotating the whole robot until the camera centers on the goal, spins the flywheel to its target RPM, and shoots. This is all four loops from above, run in order.

Example 1. In autonomous, Theo programs Sparky to drive to the shooting spot and stop. He tries to do it with the flywheel's motor encoders, counting how far the drive motors turn. On the practice field the robot ends up in a different place every match, sometimes short, sometimes off to the side. What sensor is he using wrong, and what should "drive to a spot" use instead?

Why motor encoders are the wrong tool here. A motor encoder counts how fast a drive motor spins. But wheels slip on the tiles, and a spinning motor does not tell you where the robot actually is on the field. So counting motor turns is a guess about position, and the guess drifts. That is really an open-loop move for position, from Chapter 3: nothing is measuring the true field position.

The fix: use two-wheel odometry. Odometry from Chapter 5 uses two unpowered tracking wheels plus the IMU to measure where the robot really is (X and Y in inches), even when the drive wheels slip. Feed that to Pedro Pathing, which plans a smooth profile to the target spot and corrects with feedforward plus PID the whole way. Now the loop measures true position and closes on the exact spot, match after match.

Aha: match the sensor to the quantity. "Where is the robot on the field" is odometry's job, not the motor encoder's. Motor encoders are for motor speed (flywheel RPM) and mechanism position (an arm or slide), not for driving to a spot.

Common pitfalls:
  • Using motor encoders to drive to a spot. Motor encoders count motor turns, not field position, so slipping wheels make them drift. Driving to a spot uses two-wheel odometry with Pedro Pathing.
  • Expecting one big controller to do everything. Autonomous is several small loops running together, each with its own target and sensor, not one giant loop. Build and tune them one at a time.
  • Looking for a turret. There is no turret. The shooter is fixed. Aiming means rotating the whole robot until the camera, which sits under the shooter, centers on the goal.
  • Calling it closed loop when nothing is measured. "Drive forward for one second at half power" and hoping is open loop, from Chapter 3. It is not autonomous control unless a sensor is measuring and the robot is correcting.
  • Skipping the sense step. If Sparky shoots without reading the obelisk pattern first, it may shoot the wrong artifacts. Sense, then decide, then act, in that order.

Part B: Wrap-up, the whole map in plain words

You started this course being told a secret: you already do control theory every match. Now you have the names and the reasons. Here is the whole journey in a few plain sentences.

What control is, in one line

Control is making a machine reliably do what you want, even when the world is messy. Every controller in this course is the same four-step idea from Chapter 4, repeated fast: pick a target, measure what is really happening, find the error (target minus measurement), and correct. Then do it again. That loop is the entire subject. Everything else is just smarter ways to choose the correction.

The controller family, from simple to strong

The middle of the course was one growing family of controllers. Each one adds a single new idea to the one before it. Read the ladder from the bottom up. Each rung can do a little more than the rung below it. Off to the side are the other controllers you met that are not part of the PID family.

  • Bang-bang (Chapter 6): full power on below the target, off above it. Simple and cheap, but it always ripples around the target and never settles exactly.
  • Hysteresis (Chapter 7): bang-bang with a gap (a deadband) between the on and off points, so it stops flicking on and off rapidly. The ripple band is a little wider, but the chattering stops.
  • Proportional, P (Chapter 8): size the push to the error. Big error, big push. Much smoother, but under a steady pull like gravity it settles a little short.
  • Derivative, PD (Chapter 10): add a brake that reacts to how fast the error is changing. It eases off early, so there is less overshoot and wobble (Chapter 9). Its cost is that it reacts to sensor noise.
  • Integral, PI (Chapter 11): add a memory that sums past error, so even a small leftover miss builds into a push big enough to close the gap. Its cost is windup.
  • PID (Chapter 12): all three together. P for the present error, I for the past, D for the predicted change. The workhorse of FTC control.
  • Feedforward, PIDF (Chapter 13): predict the output you will need and apply it up front, without waiting for an error. Feedback then corrects the rest. This is what makes the flywheel both fast and accurate.

And off to the side, three more controllers you met that are not part of the PID family:

  • Take-Back-Half (Chapter 15): a simple speed controller for flywheels that settles with almost no tuning, by taking back half its output change each time the error crosses zero.
  • Motion profiling and Pedro Pathing (Chapter 16): plan a smooth target path over time (speed up, cruise, slow down), then let feedforward drive along it and PID correct. This is how Sparky drives to a spot.
  • Cascade (Chapter 17): one loop inside another, where an outer loop's output becomes the inner loop's target.

The one warning: more gain is not always better

From Chapter 14: Tuning, the big lesson was that cranking a knob higher does not always help. Too little P is sluggish, but too much P oscillates. Too much I overshoots and winds up. Too much D gets jittery from noise. Good control is about balance, not brute force. And you do not always need the fanciest controller: an intake gate can stay on bang-bang, while a flywheel earns PIDF. From Chapter 18: Recipe Per Mechanism, the rule was to match the controller to the job and the load. Name what the mechanism fights, then pick the smallest controller that beats it.

The four questions you can now answer

Here is the promise from the concept map, kept. You can now look at any robot behavior, on Sparky or on another team's robot, and answer these four questions in plain words:

  • What is the target? The RPM, the heading in degrees, the spot on the field in inches, or the off-center amount you want to reach.
  • What is the sensor measuring? The motor encoder for flywheel speed, odometry for field position, the IMU for heading, the camera for how far off-center the goal is.
  • What is the error? The target minus the measurement, the gap the robot is trying to close.
  • How does the robot correct itself? Which controller from the ladder it uses to turn that error into a motor command.
The takeaway: if you can answer those four questions about the flywheel, the camera aim, or autonomous driving, you understand control theory well enough to explain it. Not the heavy math, the ideas. That was the whole goal.

  • Full path following: instead of one straight drive, the robot follows a smooth curved path across the field, correcting position and heading the whole way. Pedro Pathing does this for mecanum; Road Runner is another popular tool.
  • Better localization: fusing odometry, the IMU, and camera AprilTag sightings so the robot always knows exactly where it is, even after a bump.
  • State machines: the clean way to write "do step 1, then step 2, then step 3" for a whole autonomous routine, so each phase hands off to the next.

Practice

P1. During autonomous, Sparky must drive to a shooting spot 60 inches out and stay pointed the right way the whole time, with no driver. Name the two feedback loops it runs at the same time on the drivetrain. For each loop, give the target, the sensor, and the quantity that sensor measures.

Answer

Loop 1, drive to the spot. Target: the spot on the field, 60 inches out. Sensor: two-wheel odometry (two tracking wheels plus the IMU). Quantity measured: the robot's actual position on the field (X and Y in inches). Pedro Pathing plans a smooth profile to the spot and corrects with feedforward plus PID.

Loop 2, heading. Target: the right heading, say 0 degrees. Sensor: the IMU (gyro). Quantity measured: the robot's heading in degrees. The loop adds a small steer correction (PD) whenever the measured heading drifts off target.

Pattern, one loop per quantity. To control both position and heading, run one loop per quantity, each with the sensor that measures that quantity. Notice neither one uses the motor encoders: those are for flywheel speed and mechanism position, not field position.

P2. A rookie on another team says, "Our autonomous just runs the drive motors at half power for two seconds to reach the shooting spot. It is closed loop because the robot moves on its own." Is that closed loop or open loop? Explain in two or three sentences, and say what would make it truly closed loop.

AnswerIt is open loop, from Chapter 3. Nothing is being measured and nothing is being corrected. The robot sends power and hopes, so a low battery or a bumpy tile changes where it ends up. To make it closed loop, add a sensor and a correction: use two-wheel odometry to measure where the robot really is and let Pedro Pathing drive to the target spot, correcting the whole way.

P3. Walk a teammate through Sparky's autonomous as sense, decide, act. The obelisk is showing a green-purple-green pattern. For each of the three beats, say what happens and which sensor or controller is doing the work.

Answer

Sense. The camera reads the obelisk AprilTag and reports the pattern: green-purple-green. This is the camera reading a label, from Chapter 5. It is a measurement, not a controller.

Decide. Sparky's code sees the green-purple-green pattern and chooses to shoot the matching green-purple-green artifacts. This is a plain logic choice, no feedback loop.

Act. Sparky drives to the shooting spot with Pedro Pathing over two-wheel odometry, holding heading with the IMU (PD). At the spot, the flywheel loop (PIDF, from Chapter 19) holds the target RPM, and the aim loop (P or PD on the camera, from Chapter 20) rotates the whole robot until the goal is centered. Then Sparky shoots.

Pattern, sense then decide then act. Every robot behavior follows these three beats. Naming the sensor and controller at each beat is how you explain any autonomous routine.

Glossary

Quick definitions, grouped by layer of the concept map.

Foundations

Control system
Anything that makes a machine do what you want, like reach a distance, hold an angle, or spin at a set speed.
Setpoint (target)
The value you want, such as 3000 RPM for the flywheel or 90 degrees for a turn.
Measurement
What a sensor says is actually happening right now, like the real wheel speed.
Error
The setpoint minus the measurement: how far off you are. The controller's whole job is to shrink this to zero.
Open loop
Send a command and hope, with no sensor checking the result. Rare in FTC because the robot never knows if it worked.
Closed loop
Measure, compare to the target, and correct. This is how almost all FTC control works.
Feedback
Feeding the measurement back to compare against the target, so the robot can correct itself.
Actuator
The part that does the work, like a motor spinning the flywheel or turning the drivetrain.
Disturbance
Anything that pushes the robot off target, like an artifact hitting the flywheel or an uneven floor tile.
Artifact
The 5-inch plastic ball in DECODE that Sparky shoots into the goal. Always call it an artifact, not a sphere.
Two-wheel odometry
Two unpowered tracking wheels plus the IMU that together measure the robot's position on the field (X and Y). Used to drive to a spot.
IMU (gyro)
A sensor that measures the robot's heading, the direction it is facing in degrees.
Motor encoder
A sensor on a motor that counts rotation, giving motor speed (flywheel RPM) and mechanism position (arm or slide).
Camera and AprilTag
The robot's eyes. The Limelight camera measures how far off-center the goal is; an AprilTag is a printed marker the robot reads, like the DECODE obelisk pattern. On Sparky the camera sits under the shooter, so a centered camera means an aimed shooter.

The On/Off and PID family

Bang-bang control
The simplest closed loop: full power below target, off (or reverse) above. Robust, but it always ripples around the target.
Hysteresis (deadband)
Bang-bang with two thresholds and a gap between them, so the output does not chatter rapidly on and off. The gap is the deadband.
Proportional control (P)
Correction sized to the error: far off means push hard, close means push gently.
Gain
A tuning number that sets how strongly the controller reacts. Too small is sluggish; too large causes oscillation.
Overshoot
Going past the target before settling, like a turn that swings to 95 degrees on its way to 90.
Oscillation
Wobbling back and forth around the target instead of settling.
Steady-state error
A small leftover gap that never closes, often from a constant pull like gravity. P alone usually leaves one.
Derivative control (D)
Reacts to how fast the error is changing and acts as a brake to reduce overshoot. Downside: it amplifies sensor noise.
Integral control (I)
Adds up past error and pushes harder until the leftover gap is gone. Downside: it can wind up and overshoot.
Windup
When the integral term builds up too much while the robot is stuck, then overshoots once it frees up.
PID
Proportional plus Integral plus Derivative: present error, past error, and predicted change working together.
Feedforward (F)
A predicted output applied before any error appears, based on what the task needs. Feedback (PID) then cleans up the rest.
kS, kV, kA, kG
Feedforward gains: kS overcomes static friction to start moving, kV sets output for a target speed (great for flywheels), kA for a target acceleration, kG holds an arm or vertical slide against gravity.
PIDF
PID plus a feedforward term. Common in FTC for velocity control like the flywheel and for gravity-loaded arms.
Tuning
Adjusting the gains to get a fast, stable response. More gain is not always better.
Rise time and settling time
How fast the robot first reaches the target, and how long until it stays there without wobbling.

Other controllers

Take-Back-Half (TBH)
A simple velocity controller popular for flywheels. It sums the error and, each time the error crosses zero, takes back half of its output change, settling on a steady speed with almost no tuning.
Motion profile
A planned smooth path for a target over time: ramp the speed up, cruise, then ramp down (a trapezoid). Feedforward follows the plan, PID corrects the small error.
Path following (Pedro Pathing)
Driving the mecanum robot along a planned curve to a target spot. Pedro Pathing is the FTC library Sparky uses to do this, combining a motion profile, feedforward, and feedback.
Cascade control (nested loops)
One loop inside another: an outer position loop commands a velocity, and a faster inner velocity loop achieves it.

Putting it on Sparky

Mecanum drivetrain
Wheels that let the robot drive forward, strafe sideways, and rotate in place. Sparky's drivetrain.
Aiming (rotate the robot)
Because the shooter is fixed and the camera sits under it, Sparky aims by rotating its whole body until the camera sees the goal centered.
Linear slide
A mechanism that extends in a straight line (a lift). Vertical slides fight gravity and need a kG feedforward; horizontal slides do not.
Flywheel RPM control
Holding the shooter wheel at a target speed so every artifact flies the same distance, even right after an artifact slows the wheel.
Autonomous
The first 30 seconds when the robot drives and shoots on its own using sensors and AprilTags, no driver.
